### Ch 1 Partitions, p 1-1 — Gypsum Wallboard Partitions [092000]
- **Key terms**:
  - **Partition**: an interior wall that divides space. It also supports doors, glazing and woodwork, connects to ceilings, holds electrical and plumbing, and serves as the base for finishes.
  - **Gypsum wallboard**: a gypsum core between sheets of heavy paper (or other facings), made in standard-size sheets and installed dry. It is the most common and most versatile partition type.
  - **Wallboard components / "gyp board"**: the term can mean a whole assembly: framing, wallboard, trim at corners and edges, and joints finished with tape and joint compound.
  - **Mastic application**: gluing wallboard directly to a substrate with adhesive instead of fastening it to framing.
- **Main points**:
  - Common partition types: gypsum wallboard, lath and plaster, masonry, and glass block. The MasterFormat number for gypsum wallboard is 092000.
  - Wallboard is the most common material for partitions and ceilings in both residential and commercial work. Some newer boards use fiber from recycled newspaper.
  - Wallboard can go on studs, on furring over other walls, directly on rough walls or columns, or on suspended ceiling framing. It can be glued with mastic to concrete block and similar substrates.
  - Surface requirements for mastic:
    - Every surface must be smooth, flat, and free of oil, grease and paint.
    - Concrete must have no ridges, fins, depressions or high spots.
    - Masonry must be smooth, with no mortar sticking out.
    - Gluing onto another layer of wallboard works best.
  - The finished assembly is a smooth surface ready for paint, wallcovering or another decorative finish.

### Ch 1, p 1-2 — Gypsum Wallboard (sizes, thicknesses, edges)
- **Key terms**:
  - **Shaft wall (core) board**: a special 1 in (25 mm) board, 2 ft (600 mm) wide, used to enclose shafts.
  - **Tapered edge**: the most common edge. It runs along the long side and is recessed so tape and compound fit without leaving a bulge.
  - **Square edge**: used where appearance does not matter, for base layers in two-layer systems, and under veneer plaster.
- **Main points**:
  - Panel widths are 4 ft and 4½ ft (1200 and 1372 mm). Lengths are 8, 10, 12 and 14 ft. Contractors use the longest practical length to reduce joints.
  - Thicknesses are ¼, ⅜, ½, ⅝ and ¾ in (6.4, 9.5, 12.7, 15.9, 19 mm).
    - A single layer of ¾ in board carries a 2-hour rating, so a 2-hour partition can be one layer instead of two.
  - Typical uses by thickness:
    - ⅝ in: most commercial and high-quality residential work.
    - ½ in: residential, plus some commercial uses such as furred walls.
    - ⅜ in: some double-layer systems and remodeling (applied over existing finishes).
    - ¼ in: curved surfaces, and new finishes over old wall or ceiling surfaces.
  - Double-layer systems are used when extra fire resistance or acoustic performance is needed.
  - **Table 1.1, Wallboard Thickness Based on Frame Spacing** (16 in / 406 mm vs 24 in / 610 mm on center):

    | Application | 16 in o.c. | 24 in o.c. |
    |---|---|---|
    | Partitions | ½ in* | ⅝ in |
    | Ceilings | ½ in* | ⅝ in |
    | Furring | ½ in | ½ in |
    | Mastic applied (no frame) | ⅜ in | ⅜ in |

    *A required fire rating may call for ⅝ in Type X instead.
  - Besides standard paper-faced board, several other types exist (covered on the next pages).

### Ch 1, p 1-3 (page number not visible) — Wallboard Types
- **Key terms**:
  - **Fire-rated gypsum wallboard (Type X)**: board with a special core containing mineral additives that improve fire resistance. It is used wherever a fire-rated partition or covering is required.
  - **Water-resistant wallboard**: a backing for adhesive-set ceramic tile and similar finishes in damp areas, or where the board may get wet during construction. It also comes fire-rated.
  - **Abuse resistance**: how well a material or assembly resists three kinds of damage: (1) surface scratching or denting, (2) penetration into the stud cavity, and (3) security breaches through the whole partition.
  - **Abuse-resistant wallboard**: specialty board designed to resist those kinds of damage.
  - **Sound-deadening wallboard**: board or assemblies that reduce sound transmission.
  - **Mold-resistant wallboard**: board whose paper facing is replaced, on one or both sides, with an inorganic facing (often fiberglass mat) so mold has nothing organic to feed on.
- **Main points**:
  - For heavy-duty commercial showers and continuously wet areas, use ceramic tile on a portland cement plaster setting bed or a glass mesh mortar unit (see Ch 10). Most showers use cementitious panels instead of gypsum board.
  - Where abuse-resistant board is used:
    - Resisting surface damage: hotel lobbies, restaurant dining rooms, waiting rooms, classrooms.
    - Resisting penetration: hospital corridors, gymnasiums, locker rooms, around loading docks.
    - Security partitions are covered in Ch 14.
  - Ways manufacturers make board abuse-resistant:
    - Paper fibers or other additives in the core.
    - Abrasion-resistant face papers and heavy back liners.
    - Thin fiberglass or Lexan sheets on the back.
    - A coat of veneer plaster.
    - Any combination of these.
  - Impact resistance ranges from 60 ft-lbf (one layer of ⅝ in Type X) to over 2100 ft-lbf (a ⅝ in proprietary product with a 0.080 in Lexan backing). Set the required protection level before choosing a product.
  - Sound-deadening options:
    - Simplest: add a second ¼ or ⅜ in (6 or 10 mm) layer to a standard layer.
    - A proprietary product puts a viscoelastic polymer core between two high-density gypsum layers. The core damps sound the way the PVB interlayer does in laminated glass.
    - Multi-layer products come in thicknesses from ½ in (13 mm) to 1⅜ in (35 mm).
    - Acoustics are covered in Ch 11.
  - Mold-resistant board comes in standard and fire-rated versions.

### Ch 1, p 1-4 — Detailing Curved Partitions with Gypsum Wallboard
- **Key terms**:
  - **Dry bending**: bending the board gently, usually installed horizontally, as it is fastened to closely spaced studs.
  - **Wet bending**: wetting the board face so it bends onto the studs. It regains its original hardness when it dries.
  - **Minimum radius**: the tightest curve a board can take. It depends on board thickness and whether the board is bent wet or dry.
- **Main points**:
  - Curves are usually built from multiple layers of ¼ in (6.4 mm) or ⅜ in (9.5 mm) board.
  - **Table 1.2, Minimum Bending Radii for Standard Gypsum Wallboard** (source: US Gypsum):

    | Bending | Thickness | Long side ⊥ framing | Long side ∥ framing |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Dry | ¼ in | 5 ft (1500 mm) | 15 ft (4600 mm) |
    | Dry | ⅜ in | 7.5 ft (2290 mm) | 25 ft (7600 mm) |
    | Dry | ½ in | 20 ft (6100 mm) | — |
    | Dry | ½ in (two ¼ in layers) | 5 ft (3050 mm)* | — |
    | Wet | ¼ in | 2 ft (600 mm) | — |
    | Wet | ⅜ in | 3 ft (900 mm) | — |
    | Wet | ½ in | 4 ft (1200 mm) | — |
    | Wet | ½ in (two ¼ in layers) | 2 ft (600 mm) | — |

    *As printed; 3050 mm is actually 10 ft, so check this figure.
  - Wet-bending values assume board on a 4 in (100 mm) partition. All values are approximate; follow the manufacturer's numbers.
  - Some manufacturers make flexible ¼ in panels that bend to much tighter radii, wet or dry (see Table 1.3).

### Ch 1, p 1-5 — Curved Partitions (cont.) / Type X Composition / Wallboard Types / Framing
- **Key terms**:
  - **Hydrous calcium sulfate**: what gypsum is made of. It is about 50% water by volume.
  - **Water of crystallization**: the water bound in gypsum. In a fire it turns to steam, which absorbs heat and slows the fire.
  - **Foil-backed wallboard**: board with a foil back that keeps water vapor out of exterior wall and ceiling spaces. The foil side faces the framing.
  - **Prefinished gypsum wallboard**: board with vinyl wallcovering already applied, used in demountable partition systems.
- **Main points**:
  - **Table 1.3, Minimum Bending Radii for Flexible Gypsum Wallboard** (radius; maximum stud spacing):

    | Bend | Lengthwise | Widthwise |
    |---|---|---|
    | Dry, inside | 32–46 in; studs ≤ 9 in | 20–34 in; studs ≤ 9 in |
    | Dry, outside | 32–34 in; studs ≤ 9 in | 11–18 in; studs 6–8 in |
    | Wet, inside | 20–34 in; studs ≤ 9 in | 10–12 in; studs ≤ 6 in |
    | Wet, outside | 14–15 in; studs ≤ 6 in | 7–8 in; studs 5–6 in |

    Confirm exact limits with the manufacturer.
  - Why gypsum resists fire:
    - Turning its water to steam takes time and a lot of heat, so the board stays incombustible and insulates the unexposed side.
    - Pure gypsum shrinks as it loses water, then cracks and lets fire through.
    - Type X adds vermiculite, glass fiber and other additives to offset shrinkage and improve durability.
  - Foil-backed board is used when remodeling exterior walls and ceilings, not in interior construction.
  - Prefinished board installation:
    - Panels slip into top and bottom runners, and concealed clips join neighboring edges.
    - A thin vertical joint shows where panels meet.
    - On large projects the wallcovering can be custom-made in the type, pattern and color the designer specifies.
  - Framing materials:
    - Vertical framing (walls and furring) is wood or metal. Ceiling framing is covered in Ch 2.
    - Wood is used in residential work and occasionally small commercial projects.
    - Metal studs dominate commercial work because they are noncombustible, light and easy to work with.
    - Residential contractors prefer wood because the walls can also carry loads.

### Ch 1, pp 1-6 & 1-7 — Wood Framing / Metal Framing / Special Framing (Fig 1.1)
- **Key terms**:
  - **Metal framing**: light-gage galvanized steel formed into many sizes and shapes.
  - **Gage**: the thickness of steel studs. A higher number means thinner steel.
  - **Runners (tracks)**: C-shaped channels at the floor and top, the same width as the studs, with no flanges or prepunched holes.
  - **Dimpled metal studs**: studs rolled with closely spaced dimples. Dimpling work-hardens the steel, so a stud of the same strength uses less steel.
  - **Special framing**: specially shaped metal studs for particular partition types.
  - **Slip joint**: a top-track detail that lets the structure above deflect without loading the partition.
  - **H-shaped stud**: a stud used in area separation walls to create a firebreak between neighboring apartments or condominiums.
- **Main points**:
  - Wood framing:
    - Studs are 2×4 (actual 1½ × 3½ in; 38.1 × 88.9 mm).
    - Spacing is 16 in (406 mm) or 24 in (610 mm) on center. 16 in is more common in residential work.
    - These spacings divide evenly into 4 ft panel widths and 8, 10 and 12 ft lengths.
    - Ceiling board attaches to joists or rafters, also typically 16 in o.c.
  - Loadbearing vs nonloadbearing:
    - Metal stud partitions are usually nonloadbearing.
    - Loadbearing walls use heavy-gage structural steel studs sized by a structural engineer. Wallboard attaches to them the same way.
  - Stud thicknesses:
    - 25 gage (0.0175 in / 0.455 mm) is the most common for studs.
    - 22 gage is 0.0270 in (0.69 mm). 20 gage is 0.0329 in (0.836 mm).
    - Heavier gages are used for very tall partitions, unusual loads, framing at door openings, exterior walls, and heavy loads such as cabinets.
    - Structural steel studs come in 12, 14, 16 and 18 gage.
  - Stud shape and sizes:
    - Studs are C-shaped with small flanges and prepunched openings for conduit and wiring.
    - Depths are 1⅝, 2½, 3⅝, 4 and 6 in (41.3, 63.5, 92.1, 101.6, 152.4 mm). Flange width is about 1¼ in (32 mm).
  - Stud spacing and installation:
    - Studs go 16 or 24 in o.c. 24 in is common for nonloadbearing walls because it is cheaper and faster.
    - Runners are fastened to the floor and upper structure first. Studs are then slipped in and fixed with self-tapping screws or a crimping tool.
  - Stud depth depends on partition height:
    - 2½ in (63.5 mm) is the most common size, and 3⅝ in (92.1 mm) is also widely used.
    - 2½ in is enough for normal 8–10 ft ceilings and leaves room for electrical boxes, wiring and small pipes.
    - Deeper studs may be needed for larger pipes, recessed items like medicine cabinets, or pocket doors.
    - Table 1.4 lists maximum partition heights by stud depth.
  - Dimpled studs make screws easier to drive, slightly reduce sound transmission, and improve fire-test results. Their limiting heights are about 15–25% higher than plain studs of the same thickness.
  - Special framing (Fig 1.2):
    - Shaft wall liners for fire stairs, elevators and dumbwaiters. Board goes on one side only and gives a 2-hour separation, as codes require.
    - H-studs for area separation walls.
    - Other types: proprietary top tracks for slip joints, tracks that fasten partitions to existing ceiling grids, and flexible runners for curved partitions.
    - Slip joints are required where a fire-rated partition meets the structural floor above, so the floor can deflect without cracking the board.
  - **Fig 1.1, Metal Stud Wall Framing**:
    - (a) Standard: top runner, studs, bottom runner.
    - (b) Proprietary top track: slotted holes at 8 in (203 mm) o.c. and a ceiling grid. Wallboard runs above the track for a 1-hour partition, on one side only.

### Ch 1, pp 1-8 & 1-9 — Table 1.4 / Special Wallboard Studs (Fig 1.2) / Wood and Metal Furring
- **Key terms**:
  - **L/120, L/240, L/360**: deflection limits, where L is stud length. A limit of L/240 means the wall may bend no more than its length divided by 240.
  - **Furring**: smaller framing members (wood or metal) that only provide a base for attaching wallboard to one side of other construction, where direct attachment is not suitable.
  - **Metal furring (hat) channel**: the most common metal furring, named for its cross-section. It is screwed to concrete block, ceiling framing and similar surfaces.
  - **Resilient channel**: like a hat channel, but with legs of unequal length so only one edge touches the framing. This reduces sound transmission.
- **Main points**:
  - **Table 1.4, Maximum Partition Heights Based on Stud Depth** (ft-in):

    | Stud depth | L/120, 25 ga | L/120, 20 ga | L/240, 25 ga | L/240, 20 ga |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | 1⅝ in | **8'9"** | 11'2" | 7'11" | 8'11" |
    | 2½ in | **11'3"** | 15'1" | 10'7" | 11'9" |
    | 3⅝ in | **13'6"** | 19'1" | 13'5" | 15'2" |
    | 4 in | **14'3"** | 20'11" | 14'2" | 16'7" |
    | 6 in | **15'0"** | 27'5" | 15'0" | 21'9" |

    Bold values are the most common.
  - Table 1.4 assumptions:
    - 5 psf (240 Pa) load, studs 24 in o.c., one layer of board on each side.
    - Values are conservative industry figures and vary with ASTM C754 version and local code.
  - Deflection limits:
    - L/120 is the maximum most codes allow.
    - L/240 is the recommended maximum for brittle finishes such as veneer plaster. Some manufacturers recommend L/360.
  - **Fig 1.2, Special Wallboard Studs**:
    - (a) Proprietary stud for shaft liners: 1 in shaft wall liner, ½ in Type X board, finish side vs shaft side.
    - (b) H-stud for area separation walls: 2×4 stud, 1 in sound attenuation insulation, rated STC 50.
  - Top tracks (Fig 1.9):
    - One manufacturer's punched top track has holes for studs 8 in (203 mm) o.c. and works for both 1-hour and 2-hour partitions.
    - Another uses long-leg tracks with slotted holes for slip joints.
    - One partition variation attached to the ceiling grid is the only system where the grid passes through a wall that still carries a 1-hour rating.
  - Hinged track for curves: one manufacturer makes an adjustable track with hinged sections that curves to almost any radius and is screwed in place. A matching track is available for vertical arched openings.
    - Specialty products cost more than standard parts but can lower total cost by cutting labor.
  - Furring:
    - Furring can be screwed to masonry or concrete, or be free-standing to cover up construction. For example, a square column can be built around a rough structural column.
    - Wood furring is at least 1×2 (actual ¾ × 1½ in; 19.1 × 38.1 mm). Use 2×2 if more depth is needed.
    - There are three common types of metal furring.
    - Channels are usually spaced 16 or 24 in o.c.

### Ch 1, pp 1-10 & 1-11 — Furring (Fig 1.3) / Standard Trim [092116] (Fig 1.4) / Control Joints / Finishing
- **Key terms**:
  - **Z-furring channel**: provides more depth than standard furring channels.
  - **Standard trim**: prefabricated trim that hides or finishes the rough cut edges of wallboard.
  - **Corner bead**: L-shaped trim with legs about 1 in (25 mm) long, used on all exposed outside corners. It is covered with compound and sanded.
  - **LC bead**: trim for places where the board edge is exposed. It must be finished with compound, must go on before the board, and gives a clean edge that does not look like metal. Contractors sometimes call it U bead or J bead.
  - **L bead**: like LC bead but without the back flange, so it can go on after the board. It suits remodeling and trim next to millwork or door frames, and it is finished with compound.
  - **U bead**: trim that fits over the board edge and needs no finishing. The metal stays visible, so it is used where appearance does not matter or finishing costs must be kept low.
  - **LK bead**: like LC bead, but with a small V edge that fits into a kerf (slot) in adjacent construction, usually wood. It suits ⅜, ½ and ⅝ in board and is used where wood trim is not perfectly plumb.
  - **Control joint**: a joint used in large expanses of wallboard where movement is expected.
  - **Proprietary gypsum wallboard trim**: special trim, usually extruded aluminum, for reveals, rounded edges and other details that standard trim cannot handle.
  - **Reinforcing tape**: paper or fiberglass tape bedded in joint compound over joints.
- **Main points**:
  - **Fig 1.3, Common Types of Furring**:
    - (a) 1×2 wood, ¾ in thick.
    - (b) Hat channel, ⅞ in (22 mm).
    - (c) Resilient channel, ½ in (13 mm).
    - (d) Z-furring, 1, 1½, 2 and 3 in (25, 38, 51, 76 mm).
  - Resilient channels avoid a rigid connection between the board and the stud or ceiling framing, which reduces sound transmission.
  - Z-furring provides more depth, for example to leave room for insulation or conduit (Fig 1.3(d)).
  - Trim materials:
    - Galvanized steel is the most common.
    - Aluminum trim comes in many shapes for special detailing.
    - Vinyl trim also exists.
    - There are **six common trim shapes**.
  - **Fig 1.4, Standard Wallboard Trim Types**: (a) corner bead, (b) LC bead, (c) L bead, (d) U bead, (e) LK bead, (f) control joint.
  - Control joints matter less indoors for two reasons: interiors see little expansion and contraction, and most wall areas are limited by room size.
    - They are needed in very long corridors, large rooms, or where the building has an expansion joint.
    - Spacing reads as "not to exceed 50 ft (15 m)" of unbroken surface, but the photo is hard to read. Check it: industry sources often cite 30 ft (9 m) for walls.
  - Fig 1.5 shows four examples of proprietary trim.
  - Finishing sequence:
    1. Screw or nail the board up.
    2. Fill the joints with compound and bed the tape in it.
    3. Trowel smooth, let dry, then apply another coat feathered wider.
    4. Sand, then repeat.
  - The same three-coat process covers nail and screw holes, without tape. Once finished, the surface can be primed and painted.

### Ch 1, pp 1-12 & 1-13 — Proprietary Trim (Fig 1.5) / Fire & Sound Ratings / Levels of Finish / Code Requirements
- **Key terms**:
  - **Textured coating**: an alternative to a smooth finish, applied before painting. Contractors often prefer it because it hides minor flaws. "Orange peel" is one of the most common textures.
  - **Fire rating (1-, 2-, 3-, 4-hour)**: how long a partition stops fire and smoke from passing through, as measured by an independent lab using a standard test fire.
  - **STC (Sound Transmission Class)**: a single number that averages a partition's resistance to sound transmission across a range of frequencies. Higher is better.
  - **Levels of gypsum board finish**: standard finish levels 0–5, defined in *Recommended Levels of Gypsum Board Finish* (Gypsum Association and industry groups).
- **Main points**:
  - **Fig 1.5, Proprietary Wallboard Trim**:
    - (a) Outside and inside radius forms.
    - (b) Reveal at a door or opening trim.
    - (c) Reveal in the field of a partition.
    - (d) W trim at the ceiling.
  - Gypsum is inherently fire resistant. It is also dense, which makes it a good sound barrier.
  - Uses by fire rating:
    - 1-hour partitions: most common for interiors. They separate corridors from lease spaces, houses from attached garages, and some occupancy types.
    - 2-hour partitions: separate one occupancy type from another and enclose vertical shafts (stairs, elevators).
    - 3- or 4-hour partitions: some occupancy separations.
  - Gypsum board can also fireproof columns and beams. Fire ratings are covered in Ch 19.
  - Sound ratings come from lab tests and are published with the fire ratings.
  - **Levels of finish** (testable; finish level matters because strong raking light, such as a window perpendicular to the wall, shows flaws):

    | Level | Treatment | Typical use |
    |---|---|---|
    | 0 | No taping, finishing or accessories | — |
    | 1 | Tape bedded in compound at joints and inside corners | Plenums above ceilings; areas not normally seen |
    | 2 | Level 1 plus one coat over joints, corners, fastener heads and accessories | Water-resistant board as a tile base; areas where looks aren't critical |
    | 3 | Two coats | Heavy or medium texture; heavy-grade wallcoverings |
    | 4 | Three coats | Light textures, wallcoverings, or where cost matters. Gloss, semigloss and enamel paints are not recommended |
    | 5 | Level 4 plus a thin skim coat over the entire surface | Gloss, semigloss, enamel or non-textured flat paint; severe lighting |

  - **Building code requirements**:
    - The IBC and other model codes set requirements for single-ply and two-ply wallboard: framing size and spacing, board thickness, fastener type and spacing, and joint treatment.
    - Key tables are IBC 721.1(2) and 721.1(3).
    - Other references: UL *Building Material Directory*, Gypsum Association *Fire Resistance Design Manual*, and manufacturers' literature.
  - The IBC requires water-resistant board as the base for tile and wall panels in tub and shower enclosures and on water closet compartment walls.
  - Water-resistant board may **not** be used over vapor retarders, in very humid areas (saunas, steam rooms, gang showers), or on ceilings.

### Ch 1, pp 1-14 & 1-15 — Typical Partition Construction (Figs 1.6, 1.7)
- **Key terms**:
  - **Nonrated partition**: a common commercial partition, used when fire separation, sound control and security don't matter. It runs from the floor to the underside of the suspended ceiling and leaves the plenum above open.
  - **Power-actuated fasteners**: fasteners driven by an explosive charge, used to attach the bottom runner to a concrete floor.
- **Main points**:
  - Approximate STC ratings are in Table 1.6; acoustics are covered in Ch 11.
  - Figures 1.6–1.8 show the three most common wallboard partitions: one wood-frame partition (residential) and two metal-frame partitions (commercial).
  - **Fig 1.6, Standard Wood Frame Partition**:
    - 2×4 (51 × 102 mm) studs at 16 in o.c., with one layer of ½ in (13 mm) board on each side.
    - Labeled parts: top plates, sole plate, corner reinforcement tape, fire-rated floor/ceiling assembly, base as scheduled, acoustical sealant (if required).
    - A ¼–½ in (6–12 mm) gap is left at the floor and hidden by the base.
  - **Fig 1.7, Standard Floor-to-Ceiling Steel Stud Partition**:
    - Studs are 2½, 3⅝ or 4 in (64, 92, 100 mm) metal, with ½ or ⅝ in board.
    - Bottom runner: power-actuated fasteners at 24 in (600 mm) o.c.
    - Top runner: attached to the suspended ceiling grid with screws or rivets, with compressible foam tape between runner and grid.
    - The board edge at the ceiling is finished with LC trim and joint compound.
  - Studs slip between the runners and are fastened with self-tapping screws. Hanging and finishing the board completes the wall.
  - Stud depth for this partition:
    - 2½ in (63.5 mm) studs are usual for ceiling heights of 8–9 ft (2438–2743 mm).
    - 3⅝ in (92.1 mm) studs are used when the cavity needs room for pipes or recessed items.
  - Board thickness and spacing:
    - ⅝ in (15.9 mm) board is used in most cases because studs are typically 24 in (610 mm) o.c.

### Ch 1 (Partitions), pp 1-16 to 1-17 — Gypsum Wallboard Partitions / Partition Cracking in High-Rise Buildings
- **Key terms**:
  - **Slip joint**: A joint at the top of a partition that seals it to the slab but lets the structure move without cracking the wallboard.
  - **Intumescent material**: A material that swells and chars when heated, forming a barrier to smoke and fire.
  - **Top deflection track**: A proprietary top runner with intumescent material on its legs. It replaces fire-rated sealant at the head of the wall.
- **Main points**:
  - Why wallboard goes on horizontally (long lengths): fewer joints, so finishing is faster and cheaper. It also puts the joint at a convenient height, ties more studs together, and runs the board's strongest direction across the studs.
  - With a 9 ft (2745 mm) ceiling, two horizontal rows of 54 in (1372 mm) board can be used.
  - **Fig 1.8, 1-hr slab-to-slab partition**:
    - Common use: separating an exit corridor from adjacent spaces, or wherever code requires a 1-hr separation. It is also used when acoustical control is needed but fire separation is not.
    - Studs: 2-1/2, 3-5/8, or 4 in (64, 92, 100 mm) metal.
    - Board: 1/2 or 5/8 in (13 or 16 mm) fire-rated gypsum.
    - Sealing: fire-rated sealant at top and bottom, plus a slip joint at the top.
    - Optional sound-attenuation insulation.
  - All buildings move. Cracking is worst in high-rises because of structural movement, floor deflection, and expansion/contraction. Most cracking comes from partitions attached rigidly to walls, columns, beams, or slabs.
  - **Fig 1.9, slip joints under a structural floor**:
    - (a) Standard: 1/2 in (13 mm) max gap above the top of the studs and 1/2 in (13 mm) min overlap. Attach the wallboard to the studs, not to the top track.
    - (b) Proprietary slip-joint ceiling runner.
    - The deflection track allows up to 1 in (25 mm) of deflection with no sealant, spray fireproofing, or fitted gypsum needed.
  - Buildings also sway side to side under wind load. Fig 1.10 shows a joint at a window mullion, and Fig 1.11 shows a perimeter relief joint at a column or structural wall.

### Ch 1, pp 1-18 to 1-19 — Partition Cracking (cont.); 1-Hour and 2-Hour Rated Partitions; Chase Walls
- **Key terms**:
  - **Type X gypsum wallboard**: Fire-rated gypsum board, required for rated partitions.
  - **Fire damper**: A device in a duct that closes when the duct passes through a rated wall, keeping fire from spreading.
  - **Chase wall**: Two rows of studs braced together and spaced far enough apart to hold plumbing or recessed items.
- **Main points**:
  - **Fig 1.10, relief joint at a mullion**:
    - A continuous aluminum channel is attached to the vertical window-wall mullion.
    - The stud is attached to the top and bottom runners.
    - There is a 1/2 in (13 mm) min gap.
  - **Fig 1.11, relief joint at a structural wall or column**:
    - A metal runner is attached to the structure, with a gypsum wallboard trim and acoustical sealant or gasket.
    - Gap: 1/2 in (13 mm) min and 1/2 in max.
    - Resilient insulation is optional.
  - **Rules for all 1-hr rated partitions**:
    - Use Type X board and run the wall up to the structure above.
    - Seal all joints, edges, and penetrations.
    - Ducts through the wall need fire dampers.
    - Electrical boxes: only one box on one side of the wall in each stud space.
  - For sound control, put acoustical insulation in the stud space.
  - 2-, 3-, and 4-hr assemblies exist but are rare in interior work. The exception is 2-hr partitions around vertical enclosures such as stairways.
  - Rated partitions need a slip joint (Fig 1.9). The space between the slab and the top of the board must be sealed with fire-rated caulk. Voids at a fluted metal deck must be filled with fire-rated material.
  - **Fig 1.12, 2-hr partition**:
    - Two layers of Type X on each side of metal studs. Proprietary 3/4 in (19.1 mm) board in a single layer is an alternative.
    - Sits on a 2-hr floor assembly, with fire-rated sealant.
    - Electrical boxes: max 16 in² (10,323 mm²), one side only per framing space, with cracks sealed.

### Ch 1, pp 1-20 to 1-21 — Chase Wall; Soffits; Special Partition Construction
- **Key terms**:
  - **Soffit**: A lowered section of ceiling (e.g., above cabinets or lockers), framed down from the ceiling.
  - **Resilient channel**: A metal furring channel that separates the wallboard from the studs to reduce sound transmission.
  - **High-attenuation partition**: A partition built for high sound reduction, usually with a double row of studs.
  - **Shaft lining**: A 2-hr enclosure around stairs or dumbwaiters in high-rises.
- **Main points**:
  - **Fig 1.13, chase wall**:
    - Materials: 1-5/8 in (41.3 mm) metal studs and 5/8 in (16 mm) wallboard.
    - Bracing: 5/8 in gypsum gusset panels at the quarter points and center point of the partition.
    - Uses: can also recess bookcases or cabinets. If sound control is needed, add wallboard layers or resilient channels.
  - **Fig 1.14, soffit**:
    - Top runners attach to the ceiling support system, with studs 24 in (600 mm) o.c.
    - Also shown: a stud runner and corner trim.
    - Residential soffits use 2×2 or 2×4 wood instead of metal.
  - **Special partition construction** covers four needs:
    - acoustical separation
    - shaft linings (stairs, dumbwaiters)
    - column enclosures
    - fire ratings for structural elements
  - **Ways to add sound reduction to a single stud row** (Figs 1.15–1.20):
    - more wallboard layers
    - resilient channels
    - batt insulation in the stud cavity
    - a combination of these
    - proprietary clip systems (Fig 11.5)
  - High-attenuation walls (Figs 1.17, 1.18) usually need a double stud row that physically separates the two sides.
  - For any acoustic partition, the detail alone is not enough. Construction must be observed, all penetrations sealed, and proper doors and frames used.
  - **2-hr shafts**:
    - Usually part of the architectural design. Interior work needs them when a new stair is built in a high-rise for a multifloor tenant.

### Ch 1, pp 1-24 to 1-25 — STC Ratings; Coordination Items; Environmental Considerations
- **Key terms**:
  - **Blocking**: 3/4 in (19 mm) plywood or solid wood between the studs that gives screws a solid base for cabinets, paneling, and wall-hung items.
  - **Kickers**: Braces from above the ceiling to the structure that steady partitions carrying heavy doors or cabinets.
  - **Synthetic gypsum**: Gypsum made as a byproduct of other processes and chemically identical to mined gypsum.
- **Main points**:
  - **Table 1.5, STC vs. effect**:

    | STC | What you hear through the wall |
    |---|---|
    | 25 | Normal speech clearly heard |
    | 30 | Loud speech heard and understood fairly well |
    | 35 | Loud speech heard but not intelligible |
    | 42–45 | Loud speech heard only faintly; normal speech not heard |
    | 46–50 | Loud speech not audible; loud sounds other than speech faint, if heard at all |

    Music and machinery may need higher ratings. See Ch 11 for acoustics.
  - **Vertical deflection**: Runners are nailed, screwed, or fastened with power-actuated fasteners. If the structure moves, provide for it with a slip joint.
  - **Horizontal movement**: Wind moves tall buildings. A partition fixed rigidly to a mullion or flexible wall may crack.
  - **Heavy loads**: Metal-stud gypsum walls hold only moderate loads. Show blocking on the drawings for heavy items.
  - **Partition depth**: Choose a stud size that fits the largest built-in item. Very deep items need a chase wall (Fig 1.13).
  - **Anchoring**: Commercial partitions often stop at the suspended ceiling, with the top runner fixed to the grid. Heavy doors or cabinets need kickers to the floor above.
  - **Fig 1.19, 2-hr partition at a stair opening**:
    - 2-hr shaft wall construction and 1/2 in (13 mm) gypsum board on furring.
    - A J-runner on the steel beam supports the 2-hr shaft wall, with 1/2 in deflection space.
    - Use 1-5/8 in studs if the beam is deeper than 2 ft (600 mm). Add a control joint if required.
  - **Environment**:
    - Over 36 billion ft² (3.36 billion m²) of wallboard was produced in 2005.
    - Recycled paper facings have been used since the 1950s. Some boards use recycled newspaper mixed into the core, which is more rigid.
    - About 28% of the industry's gypsum is synthetic.

### Ch 1, pp 1-28 to 1-29 — Lath and Plaster Partitions [092113]; Plaster Partition Components
- **Key terms**:
  - **Flue gas desulfurization**: A power-plant scrubbing process whose byproduct is the main source of synthetic gypsum in North America.
  - **Plaster**: A cementitious material applied in a plastic state that then sets hard.
  - **Stucco**: Portland cement plaster used on a building's exterior.
  - **Keene's cement**: A mix of pure, fully dehydrated gypsum and lime putty, used where extreme abrasion resistance is needed.
  - **Scagliola**: Plaster applied and painted to look like marble.
  - **Sgraffito**: Layers of different-colored plaster, with the top layer scratched away while soft to reveal the colors below.
  - **Marezzo**: Imitation marble made of colored Keene's cement, precast on a glass or marble bed.
  - **Fresco**: Painting on freshly spread plaster before it sets.
  - **Standard lath and plaster**: The traditional method of several plaster coats applied over open lath on studs.
  - **Veneer plaster**: A very thin plaster coat, 1/16–1/8 in (1.6–3 mm), over special gypsum board lath.
  - **Diamond mesh lath**: Expanded metal lath wired to framing. It can be bent into single or double curves.
  - **Flat rib lath**: Metal lath more rigid than diamond mesh, used for flat ceilings.
  - **Rib metal lath**: Lath with 3/8 or 3/4 in (10 or 19 mm) ribs about 4 in (100 mm) apart, used for studless solid partitions or widely spaced framing.
  - **Gypsum lath ("blueboard")**: Gypsum board with a light-blue paper face made to bond with veneer plaster.
- **Main points**:
  - **Recycling old wallboard**:
    - Some plants recycle drywall if it is free of screws, nails, asbestos, and lead paint. Collection and transport cost discourages it.
    - Board can be ground to ≤1/2 in (13 mm) pieces and used as a soil additive. Farmers in California and Colorado use it for grapes, peas, and peanuts.
  - **Plaster's advantages over wallboard**:
    - forms single or double curves
    - molds into ornate pieces
    - takes many textures
    - resists abrasion
    - resists water if portland cement is used
  - **Plaster ingredients**:
    - Binders: two types, gypsum (most interiors) or portland cement (wet, steamy, or damp areas).
    - Also: lime, water, and aggregates (sand, vermiculite, perlite).
  - Where water resistance is needed, use portland cement plaster over metal lath, because moisture deteriorates gypsum plaster.
  - Pumice, perlite, or vermiculite aggregates reduce shrinkage, extend coverage, cut cost, and increase plasticity.
  - Straw or wax gives decorative effects, and pigment gives integral color.
  - Portland cement plaster can go directly on concrete or masonry, but furring plus veneer plaster is preferred.
  - Solid plaster partitions (2+ in thick) are possible but rarely built.
  - **Fig 1.21, expanded metal lath**: (a) diamond mesh, (b) flat rib, (c) rib. MasterFormat for metal and gypsum lath: [092236]. Diamond mesh also comes self-furring for column fireproofing and replastering.
  - Rated partitions must use fire-rated gypsum lath.

### Ch 1, pp 1-30 to 1-31 — Building Code Provisions for Plaster; Typical Plaster Construction; Lime Plaster [092523]
- **Key terms**:
  - **Scratch coat**: The first plaster coat, about 1/2 in (13 mm), roughened with ridges so the next coat bonds mechanically.
  - **Brown coat**: The second coat, about 1/4 in (6 mm), which levels the surface.
  - **Finish coat**: The final coat, about 1/8 in (3 mm), for smoothness and texture.
  - **Lime plaster (Venetian plaster)**: Slaked lime putty mixed with finely crushed stone, used as a surface finish.
  - **Slaked lime**: Crushed limestone heated to drive off CO₂, mixed with water ("slaked"), then ground to a fine white powder.
- **Main points**:
  - Building codes govern plaster mixes, frame spacing, lath types, and application methods. Check the model code.
  - Trim for outside corners, openings, and abutting materials is similar to wallboard trim.
  - **Three-coat plaster over wire lath (Fig 1.22)**: scratch, then brown, then finish. Two-coat systems are used mainly as a base for ceramic tile.
  - **Veneer plaster (Fig 1.23)**:
    - Assembly: one or two coats over gypsum lath, on wood or metal studs 16 or 24 in o.c. Batt insulation is optional.
    - Pros: hard, durable, faster, and cheaper, with less moisture.
    - Its one disadvantage: gypsum lath can't be the base in wet areas (showers, tubs).
    - Variations: two layers of fire-rated lath for 2 hr, plus batt insulation and resilient channels for sound.
  - For wet areas: cement backer board in residential or moderately wet work, standard lath and plaster in very wet commercial work.
  - Stud spacing depends on the lath type and weight and on the finish (e.g., tile). 2-1/2 in (63.5 mm) metal studs should be 16 in (406 mm) o.c.
  - **Table 1.7, max stud spacing, wood / metal studs** (psy = pounds per square yard of lath):

    | Lath | Wood studs | Metal studs |
    |---|---|---|
    | Diamond mesh 2.5 psy | 16 in | 12 in |
    | Diamond mesh 3.4 psy | 16 in | 16 in |
    | Flat rib 2.75 psy | 16 in | 16 in |
    | Flat rib 3.4 psy | 19 in | 19 in |
    | 3/8 in rib 3.4 psy | 24 in | 24 in |
    | 3/8 in gypsum lath | 16 in | 16 in |
    | 1/2 in gypsum lath, 1-coat | 16 in | 16 in |
    | 1/2 in gypsum lath, 2-coat | 16/24 in | 16/24 in |
    | 5/8 in gypsum lath, 1-coat | 16/24 in | 16/24 in |
    | 5/8 in gypsum lath, 2-coat | 24 in | 24 in |

    24 in spacing may need special joint treatment.
  - Lime plaster hardens by reabsorbing CO₂ and turning back into limestone. Modern mixes add small amounts of polymers and preservatives.
  - Its look depends on the colors and additives, the application method, and the applicator's skill.

### Ch 1, pp 1-32 to 1-33 — Security Partitions; Plaster Standards; Lime Plaster (cont.); Glass-Reinforced Gypsum [092713]; Masonry Partitions
- **Key terms**:
  - **Security partition**: A solid plaster wall over a heavy-gage perforated steel sheet that resists breakthrough and ballistic attack.
  - **GRG (glass-reinforced gypsum)**: High-strength gypsum reinforced with glass fibers and cast in molds. Also called **FRG** or **GFRG**.
  - **Masonry**: Brick, concrete block, glass block, structural clay tile, terra cotta, and gypsum block.
- **Main points**:
  - **Security partition**: high-strength plaster over the steel sheet, with a 3000 psi (20,684 kPa) finish coat. It is 3-1/2 to 4-1/2 in (89–115 mm) thick, lightweight, and used in jails and vaults.
  - **Plaster ASTM standards**:
    - C28: gypsum plasters
    - C35: inorganic aggregates
    - C59: casting and molding plaster
    - C61: Keene's cement
    - C150: portland cement
    - C206: finishing hydrated lime
    - C207: masonry lime
    - C587: veneer plaster
    - C631: bonding compounds
    - C841: lathing and furring installation
    - C842: interior gypsum plaster application
    - C843: veneer plaster application
    - C844: gypsum base for veneer
    - C847: metal lath
    - C897: job-mixed portland cement aggregates
  - **Lime plaster benefits**:
    - more flexible and less prone to cracking; small cracks can self-heal
    - high permeability, so less trapped water
    - high pH makes it a biocide that resists mold
    - low energy to make, releases no VOCs, and absorbs CO₂ as it sets
  - Lime plaster goes over scratch or brown coats, gypsum board, or straw bale. Over wallboard it needs a Level 4 or 5 finish. In wet areas, add a penetrating water-repellent sealer.
  - **GRG**:
    - Uses: column covers, arches, coffered ceilings, moldings, light troughs, and trim.
    - Premanufactured in molds and site-finished like plaster or wallboard.
  - **GRG design tips**:
    - Expose only one side, because the back is rough and shows ribs.
    - Minimize the number of unique molds, since more molds cost more.
    - Use it only as decorative cover, never as a bearing element.
    - Detail corners with draft angles and avoid 90° angles so castings release from the mold.
    - Attach with screws, adhesive, or hanging. Field-cut the mechanical and electrical openings.
    - Extend a curved GRG piece about 2 in (50 mm) into the flat wallboard so the joint can be floated.
    - Joint alignment tolerance ≤1/8 in (3 mm), and joint width ≤3/8 in (10 mm).
  - **Plaster coordination**:
    - Bolt mounting strips or wood cleats to metal studs for cabinets and other loads.
    - Use steel door frames of at least 16 gage.
    - Place control/relief joints where plaster abuts structure, where runs exceed 30 ft (9.1 m), and at building expansion joints. Full-height doors can serve as control joints.
    - Tile-faced walls need min 20-gage, 3-5/8 in (92.1 mm) studs at 16 in o.c. Scratch plus mortar coat for tile should be ≤1 in (25.4 mm).
  - **Masonry partitions**: interior ones are nonloadbearing and used for special purposes or to match existing work. Most are concrete block or glass block.

### Ch 1, pp 1-36 to 1-37 — Glass Block Details; Curved Glass Block; Demountable Partitions [102219]

    - Sill: asphalt emulsion.
  - **Fig 1.25, jamb and vertical joint**:
    - Jamb: steel channel with a 1 in (25.4 mm) overlap, expansion strip, and joint reinforcement.
    - Vertical joint: panel anchor attached to the vertical stiffener.
  - Fig 1.26: an intermediate stiffener behind the block. Panel anchors go to a pipe column, square or rectangular tubing, or a custom shape.
  - A Fig 1.27 detail (block rigidly attached top and bottom) can eliminate intermediate stiffeners if the local code allows.
  - **Curved walls**: the minimum radius depends on block thickness, assuming a wedge-shaped head joint of 1/8 in (3 mm) inside and 5/8 in (16 mm) outside (Table 1.9).
  - **Glass block coordination**:
    - Make opening width and height even multiples of the block size.
    - Deflection of supporting members must be ≤ L/600, verified by a structural engineer.
    - Intermediate vertical stiffeners are required at every change of direction in a multicurved wall and wherever a curve meets a straight run.
  - **Demountable vs. operable**: operable partitions (Ch 3) act like special doors that open and close often. Demountable ones stay in place.
  - **Demountable benefits**:
    - Fast reconfiguration without messy demolition.
    - Parts (doors, glazing) can be rearranged.
    - Faster initial build, because flooring, ceilings, lighting, and mechanical can finish first and the partitions go in later.
    - Higher first cost.

### Ch 1, pp 1-38 to 1-39 — Typical Demountable Partition Construction; Movable Partitions [102223]
- **Key terms**:
  - **Progressive system**: Panels must go in (and come out) in a set order. It costs less but is less flexible.
  - **Nonprogressive system**: Each panel can be removed or replaced on its own.
  - **Movable partition**: A partial- or full-height partition not attached to the building structure. It is often part of a furniture system, and the term is used interchangeably with "demountable".
- **Main points**:
  - **Fig 1.27, alternate glass block detail**: expansion joint of compressible filler and sealant at the jambs and every 8th vertical course. A synthetic rubber bond breaker is used at the base.
  - **Table 1.9, min radii for curved glass block**:

    | Block | Min inside radius |
    |---|---|
    | 4 in (100 mm) | 32 in (813 mm) |
    | 6 in (150 mm) | 48-1/2 in (1232 mm) |
    | 8 in (200 mm) | 65 in (1650 mm) |
    | 12 in (300 mm) | 98-1/2 in (2500 mm) |

  - Demountable walls have lower life-cycle cost where plans change often, thanks to savings in material and labor.
  - **Four basic demountable components**:
    - floor runners
    - ceiling runners
    - stud sections with clips
    - prefinished gypsum panels, usually vinyl-covered
  - Panels are 24 or 30 in (610 or 762 mm) wide to fit 4 or 5 ft (1220 or 1524 mm) planning modules. Small vertical joints and the top track are visible, and the base trim snaps on.
  - Manufacturers also supply door frames, glazing, bank rails, openings, and hanging shelves and furniture in the slots between panels.
  - **Bottom track**: sits on the floor. A spiked version can sit over carpet without crushing it, so the old location is less noticeable when the wall moves. The top track attaches to the suspended ceiling.

  - **Movable partition advantages**:
    - recycled materials
    - clean install with no wet joint compound

    - lower life-cycle cost where moves are frequent

    - no fire rating
    - cannot conceal plumbing
    - limits some finishes
    - limited security
    - limited support for cabinets and woodwork

### Ch 1, pp 1-40 to 1-41 — Movable Partition Details; Selecting a Movable Partition System; Constraints; Coordination
- **Key terms**:
  - **COM (customer's own material)**: A finish the client supplies for the panels.
  - **Class A flame spread**: The highest (most restrictive) flame-spread classification for finishes.
- **Main points**:
  - **Fig 1.29, movable partition details**:

    - Clips to an exposed T-runner grid, so it can be removed without damaging the ceiling.
    - Can screw into a slotted grid.
    - Can attach to a gypsum board ceiling if wood or metal backing is above.
  - Some systems ship with finish already applied. Others hang panels on a steel frame that allows interchangeable finishes (painted steel, gypsum board, wood, HPL, fabric, vinyl, glass) and carries wiring and cabling.
  - **Selection factors**:
    - how it assembles and disassembles
    - how it connects to the structure
    - panel sizes; door types and sizes; glass units
    - fit with the building module
    - angled connectors
    - finishes, including COM
    - acoustical and flame-spread ratings
    - integrated power and data
    - individual panel replacement
    - how it rests on carpet or other flooring
    - support for work surfaces and storage
    - recycled content and recyclability
    - effect on indoor air quality
    - base and top track types
    - aesthetics

### Ch 2 CEILINGS, p 2-1 — Introduction; Suspended Acoustical Ceilings [095100]; Acoustical Ceiling Components
- **Key terms**:
  - **Plenum**: The space above a suspended ceiling, used for mechanical systems, wiring, and other services.
  - **Suspended acoustical ceiling**: Thin panels of wood, mineral, or glass fiber in a metal grid hung on wires from the structure.
  - **Lay-in system**: The most common type, where panels rest on an exposed T-shaped grid.
  - **Tegular system**: A lay-in variation with rabbeted panel edges.
- **Main points**:
  - Besides being the finish surface, ceilings integrate:
    - lighting
    - partition attachment
    - supply and return air grilles and diffusers
    - speakers
  - Their detailing is critical.
  - **Three ceiling groups** by attachment (Fig 2.1):
    - suspended from the structure
    - attached directly to the structure
    - structure and finish are the same (exposed)
  - **Common methods**:
    - Residential: gypsum board screwed directly to floor or ceiling joists.
    - Commercial: a wire-suspended system with acoustical tile, gypsum board, or decorative finish. It gives a flat surface and leaves room for services in the plenum.
  - Suspended acoustical ceilings are the most common commercial type. The tiles are perforated or fissured to absorb sound, but they do not block sound transmission much.
  - **Advantages of suspended acoustical ceilings**:
    - low cost
    - fast install
    - sound control
    - flexibility
    - adapts to lighting and mechanical
    - easy plenum access

### Ch 2 (Ceilings), pp 2-4 to 2-5 — Suspended Acoustical Ceilings (cont.): Concealed Spline, Access, Tolerances
- **Key terms**:
  - **Retention clips**: clips fitted above ceiling tiles so the tiles can't be pushed up out of the grid. Required for fire-rated assemblies when retention is called for.
  - **Concealed spline ceiling**: a suspended system whose cross members fit into slots cut in the tile edges, so the grid is hidden.
  - **Downward accessible system**: a concealed spline system where an access clip or special tool pulls a two-tile unit down by pivoting it from one end.
  - **Upward accessible system**: a concealed spline system where a two-tile unit is pushed up from a pivot point and removed. The tiles rest on concealed Z-clips.
  - **Z-clips**: hidden clips that hold tiles in an upward accessible ceiling.
  - **Cardboard splines**: splines placed in all joints of a concealed spline ceiling to force the tiles into closer alignment.
- **Main points**:
  - Tiles are easy to cut for lights, sprinklers and other penetrations. Special tiles are cut for nonstandard openings.
  - The accessible percentage of a ceiling is specified. For example, 25% accessible means one-fourth of the tiles are removable two-tile units. Some manufacturers mark accessible tiles with a small pin in the corner.
  - Upward accessible is preferred over downward accessible, because the pull-down tool chips tile edges and the ceiling becomes unsightly.
  - Suspended ceilings are installed level to within 1/8 in in 12 ft (3 mm in 3660 mm). Ceilings are more level than floors, so critical dimensions are often measured from the ceiling down. Standard suspension parts can also form vertical rises and sloped sections.
  - **Sidebar, "Maintaining the Flush Appearance of Concealed Spline Ceilings"**: variations as small as 0.005 in (0.127 mm) show shadows under grazing light, so ambient lighting is preferred. Fixes are cardboard splines (they add cost and make removal harder) or bevel-edged tiles.

### Ch 2, pp 2-6 to 2-7 — Plenums, Rated Ceilings; Special Ceiling Assemblies: Sound Barriers; Seismic Restraint
- **Key terms**:
  - **Return air plenum**: the space above a suspended ceiling used to return air to the HVAC system through grilles.
  - **Plenum-rated wiring**: wiring approved to run exposed in a plenum. Otherwise, wiring must run in metal conduit.
  - **Rated acoustical ceiling system**: rated mineral tiles on a rated grid, with hold-down clips and expansion slots so the grid can expand when heated. It is part of a complete rated floor/ceiling or roof/ceiling assembly.
  - **Plenum sound barrier**: construction above a partition that stops sound from traveling over it through the plenum.
  - **Seismic restraints**: structural elements that keep nonstructural elements in place during earthquakes.
  - **Seismic design category**: an IBC rating (A–F) based on three factors: soil conditions, risk (occupancy) category, and geographic location.
  - **Risk (occupancy) categories**: IBC categories I–IV that rank how important it is for a building to survive an earthquake.
  - **Importance factor**: a multiplier assigned per risk category. Category IV is 1.50; categories I and II are 1.00.
- **Main points**:
  - When the plenum is a return air space, codes require that no combustible material be placed there and that plastic wiring run in metal conduit. Some codes allow approved plenum-rated low-voltage wiring (telephone, computer, low-voltage lighting, signal) to be exposed.
  - Suspended ceilings can be rated or nonrated. A ceiling alone cannot stop fire from spreading between floors.
  - **Sound barrier options**:
    - Cheapest: lay fire-rated sound attenuation insulation on the tiles for about 4 ft (1200 mm) on each side of the partition (Fig 2.6).
    - Better: extend the partition up to the structure, or hang a gypsum wallboard or sheet lead barrier down to the grid. These cost more but attenuate sound better. See Ch 11.
  - **Sidebar, "Building Code Requirements for Suspended Acoustical Ceilings"**: tiles must meet flame spread limits, and nearly all are Class A. Systems must be installed per ASTM C635 and C636. Fire-resistance-rated assemblies follow IBC Ch 7.
  - About half of the U.S. is at risk for seismic activity. In all areas, the ceiling and attached items must not fall. The IBC may also require restraint for partitions, cabinets, access floors, sprinkler pipes and bookcases.
  - **Soil classes**: A hard rock, B rock, C very dense soil/soft rock, D stiff soil, E soft soil, F special soil. **Class D is the default when soil is unknown.**
  - **Risk categories**: I miscellaneous, II standard occupancy, III hazardous, IV essential (hospitals, fire stations, power plants). Minor storage, small offices and retail are I or II.
  - Location comes from maps in IBC Sec. 1613 (Fig 2.7) or USGS maps, which show the maximum considered earthquake as a percentage of gravitational acceleration.
  - The seismic design category must be set by a **structural engineer** and shown on the construction documents, usually the structural cover sheet or the specifications.
  - There are **six categories, A (least restrictive) to F (most restrictive)**. Categories A and B need no special design details.
  - **Fig 2.6 Plenum Sound Barrier**: two layers of 1-1/2 in (38) fire-rated sound attenuation insulation extending 4 ft (1200), with wallboard trim and compressible foam tape.

- **Main points**:
  - **Category I (low hazard to life)**: agricultural facilities, certain temporary facilities, minor storage.
  - **Category II**: everything not in categories I, III or IV.

### Ch 2, pp 2-10 to 2-11 — Seismic Restraint (cont.); Table 2.2 Ceiling Seismic Design Requirements

  - **CISCA**: Ceilings & Interior Systems Construction Association, which publishes the seismic ceiling guides.

  - Categories D, E and F have the strictest requirements. Category C shares many of the same details but is less stringent.

### Ch 2, pp 2-12 to 2-13 — Seismic Perimeter Details; Coordination; Gypsum Wallboard Ceilings [092116]
- **Key terms**:
  - **Spacer bars**: bars that keep grid members from separating at the perimeter.
  - **Compression struts**: rigid vertical posts that limit ceiling movement in categories D, E and F.
  - **Surface-mounted items**: ceiling-mounted elements such as lights, HVAC grilles and registers, speakers, smoke detectors, sprinkler heads, fire alarms and signs. They must follow the design plan and the code.
  - **Demountable partition**: a movable partition. The ceiling above it needs a continuous slotted grid so the grid isn't marred when the top runner is removed and reinstalled.
  - **Drapery pocket**: a recess in the ceiling for drapery track or horizontal blinds.
- **Main points**:
  - **Category C perimeter**: a 3/8 in (10 mm) gap between the grid ends and the wall angle, and a wall angle at least 7/8 in (22 mm) wide.
  - **Categories D/E/F perimeter**: the grid is attached on two adjacent sides and not the opposite sides. The gap is at least 3/4 in (19 mm) and the ceiling angle at least 2 in (51 mm).
  - In both cases, spacer bars are required and hanger wires go no more than 8 in (203 mm) from the wall. Manufacturers' proprietary clips can replace these provisions.
  - In the field, 12-gage hangers are at 48 in (1220) o.c. D/E/F also need lateral bracing at 12 ft (3660) o.c. with rigid compression struts (Fig 2.10). More information is at USG's seismicceilings.com.
  - **Coordination items**: recessed lights, ductwork, sprinkler piping, fire alarm speakers, smoke detectors, drapery pockets.
  - **Recessed lights**: check clearance above the ceiling for lights, coffers and similar features (see Fig 16.2).
  - **Plenum space**: a return-air plenum allows only noncombustible materials. Even fire-retardant wood blocking is generally not permitted. Wiring must be in conduit or plenum-rated.
  - **Access**: choose a system that opens and closes without damage or soiling.
  - **Remodeling**: painting acoustical tiles is cheap but may destroy their acoustical value, so replace or clean them instead.
  - **Heavy loads**: partitions that stop at the ceiling need extra bracing for heavy loads such as bookcases. Diagonal studs are screwed through the ceiling into the top runner and secured to the structure. This also prevents shake from door slams and serves as seismic bracing in D/E/F.
  - Ceiling gypsum wallboard is 1/2 in (13 mm) or 5/8 in (16 mm) thick, depending on frame spacing.
  - **Figures**:
    - **Fig 2.8**: partition bracing for D/E/F, with 12-gauge wire and partition bracing independent of the grid bracing. Components must resist loads perpendicular to the partition face.
    - **Fig 2.9 Perimeter Closure**: 8 in (203) max to the first hanger. If alternate clips are used, spacer bars and perimeter wire aren't required (verify with the manufacturer).

### Ch 2, pp 2-14 to 2-15 — Gypsum Wallboard Ceilings: Standard Ceiling Assemblies; Applicable Standards
- **Key terms**:
  - **Furring**: framing added below joists to create a small extra space for wiring or ducts.
  - **Furring channels (hat channels)**: light metal channels attached to the main runners. Wallboard is screwed to them.
  - **Suspended gypsum wallboard ceiling**: a grid of 1-1/2 in steel channels plus furring, hung by wires from the structure. It is used in commercial work to keep clear space above.
  - **Ceiling radiation damper**: a damper required where an air diffuser penetrates a rated ceiling membrane.
- **Main points**:
  - Use 1/2 in wallboard for framing at 16 in (406) o.c. and 5/8 in for 24 in (600) o.c. Use the longest practical lengths to minimize joints.
  - When wallboard is attached directly to structure, the only other components are fasteners, tape and joint compound. Screws are used almost always because they're faster, easier, and don't pull out.
  - In residential work, wallboard is screwed directly to the joists. Larger spaces for ducts or dropped soffits are made by boxing in with wood framing.
  - **Commercial suspended assembly (Fig 2.11)**:
    - 1-1/2 in (38) steel channels at 4 ft (1200) o.c., hung by wires at 4 ft o.c.
    - furring channels at 16 or 24 in o.c., wire-tied or clipped to the channels
    - most commercial work uses 24 in (600) spacing with 5/8 in (16 mm) wallboard
  - Proprietary systems resemble T-bar grids but take screwed wallboard. They include outside and inside corners, angles and curved sections (Fig 2.12).
  - **Sidebar, "Applicable Standards for Suspended Acoustical Ceiling Systems"**:
    - ASTM C423: sound absorption test (reverberation room)
    - ASTM C635: manufacture and performance of metal suspension systems
    - ASTM C636: installation of metal suspension systems
    - ASTM E580: installation in areas subject to earthquakes
    - ASTM E1264: classification of acoustical ceiling products
    - CISCA Seismic Zones 0–2 recommendations and Zones 3 & 4 guidelines
  - **Sidebar, "Building Code Requirements for Gypsum Wallboard Ceilings"**:
    - The entire suspension system must be noncombustible, and codes set the minimum hanger wire size.
    - Installation follows GA-216 (*Application and Finishing of Gypsum Panel Products*) plus ASTM C840, C754 and C1007.
    - In a rated floor/ceiling assembly, penetrations need special details: diffusers need a radiation damper, and access doors must be rated to match.
    - In most commercial buildings the structural floor provides the rating, not the ceiling. Still, verify with the architect or building official. Seismic restraint may also apply.

### Ch 2, pp 2-16 to 2-17 — Access Panels; Custom Ceiling Assemblies; Coordination; GWB with Acoustical Ceilings; Plaster Ceilings [092113]
- **Key terms**:
  - **Access panel**: a prefabricated steel unit with a hinged door, framed into a wallboard ceiling to reach valves, junction boxes, fire dampers and similar items. A 24 in (600 mm) square is usually enough.
  - **Resilient channels**: channels attached to joists to isolate the wallboard for acoustical control in residential work.
  - **Type X gypsum wallboard**: fire-resistant wallboard required in fire-rated floor/ceiling assemblies.
  - **Cloud**: a separate acoustical tile or wallboard panel suspended below the main ceiling.
  - **Lath and plaster ceiling**: a ceiling built like a lath and plaster partition. It costs more and is harder to build than GWB but can curve in two directions.
- **Main points**:
  - Wallboard ceilings are smooth but lack the accessibility of suspended acoustical ceilings. Access panels look bad and crack at the joint compound, so limit wallboard ceilings to areas without access needs or have the engineers relocate equipment.
  - Wallboard can form stepped, sloped, coffered, vaulted and arched ceilings using framing and studs. Minimum curve radii are in Tables 1.2 and 1.3.
  - **Coordination**:
    - Type X wallboard in rated assemblies.
    - Troffers, air terminals and heavy equipment supported separately.
    - Valve and junction box locations coordinated to minimize access panels.
    - Sloped, vaulted or arched ceilings checked for duct and piping interference.

### Ch 2, pp 2-20 to 2-21 — Linear Metal (cont.); Decorative Suspended [095433]; Integrated [095800]; Abuse-Resistant [095700]; Metal and Wood Suspended Ceilings
- **Key terms**:
  - **Decorative suspended ceiling**: a ceiling hung from wires or threaded rods. The visible part can be metal baffles, wood strips, mirrored panels, open aluminum or wood grids, or proprietary systems.
  - **Integrated ceiling**: a suspended system designed to hold tile, lights, supply and return grilles, sprinklers and partition attachment in one unified way.
  - **Abuse-resistant ceiling**: a product designed to resist environmental damage, impact, frequent access, vandalism, or unauthorized entry to the ceiling space.
  - **Compression members**: struts at 48 in (1220) o.c. that resist uplift of the grid.
  - **Hold-down clips**: clips that stop incidental uplift of panels.
  - **Metal and wood suspended ceilings**: panels on a wire-hung grid, made of aluminum or other metals, wood veneer over particleboard or fiberboard cores, or wood veneer laminated to aluminum.
- **Main points**:
  - Linear metal pieces come in painted and anodized finishes. Gaps between pieces allow sound absorption and return air, and batts can be added on top. Fig 2.16 shows hanger wire, snap-on panels and a continuous wall closure strip.
  - Many decorative ceilings use 24 × 24 in (600 × 600) panels in a standard exposed T-bar grid, from simple wood or metal to historic tin looks or coffered wood. These keep plenum access.
  - **Integrated ceilings**:
    - Used where partitions and lights change often.
    - Most work on 4 ft (1200) or 5 ft (1500) planning grids.
    - Lights have plug-in connectors and HVAC uses flexible ducts, so services relocate easily. Partition top tracks attach with screws or clips.
    - They cost more but save money with frequent changes, so do a life-cycle cost analysis.
  - **Abuse-resistant ceilings**:
    - Used in schools, hospitals, industrial plants, and security or detention facilities.
    - **Light abuse** (commercial kitchens): mineral or glass-fiber tile with plastic, fiberglass, fabric, aluminum or metal-membrane faces.
    - **Heavier use**: snap-in perforated steel, stainless or aluminum panels with acoustic backing.
    - **Maximum security**: metal plank systems of heavy-gage steel pans on wall-mounted angles with tamper-resistant fasteners.
    - Where plenum access must be restricted, compression members go at 48 in o.c. Panels are held by clips, or special panels snap under a bulb tee and can't be removed without obvious damage.
    - **Plaster** systems use a hard finish coat over base coats on heavy expanded metal lath. **Gypsum** systems use multiple layers separated by heavy-gage expanded metal lath, plus a dense plaster veneer. Both resist abuse but have **poor acoustical performance**.
  - **Metal and wood suspended ceilings**:
    - Panels are solid but usually perforated for acoustics, flat or curved, and can be custom shaped with CNC cutouts.
    - Acoustical batts can go above.
    - Panels on clips or torsion springs swing out of the way for access.

### Ch 5 GLAZING, p 5-1 — Common Glass Types [088100]: Float, Heat-Strengthened, Tempered
(The left page is the end of a Ch 4 hardware finish/manufacturer table and is not relevant.)
- **Key terms**:
  - **Glazing**: installing glass in framing. The word also means the glass itself used in construction.
  - **Float glass**: the most common glass, made by floating molten glass on molten tin, then slowly cooling (**annealing**) it. It replaced plate glass by the mid-1960s.
  - **Annealed**: slowly cooled. Annealed glass is the float glass baseline.
  - **Heat-strengthened glass**: glass about twice as strong as annealed, used mostly for exterior windows.
  - **Tempered glass**: float glass heated to about 1200°F (650°C) and rapidly cooled. This puts compression on the surfaces and tension in the core. It is a safety glass that breaks into small cubical pieces.
- **Main points**:
  - Float glass comes clear or tinted (bronze, gray, green). In interiors it is used in small openings or where safety glazing isn't required.
  - Float thicknesses are 1/8, 3/16, 1/4, 3/8, 1/2, 5/8 and 3/4 in (3, 5, 6, 10, 12, 16, 19 mm). Others exist but are rarely used inside.
  - **Tempered glass is about 4× stronger in bending** than float glass of the same thickness, and its impact resistance is much better. It can be used in hazardous locations.
  - Tempered thicknesses run 1/8 to 7/8 in (3–22 mm), and 1/4 in (6 mm) is common for interiors. Maximum sizes range from 42 × 84 in for 1/8 in glass to 74 × 110 in for 7/8 in glass.

### Ch 5, pp 5-2 to 5-3 — Tempered Glass (cont.); Bow Tolerances; Laminated Glass; Table 5.1; Glazing Standards
- **Key terms**:
  - **Tong marks**: small marks near one edge where glass was held vertically by tongs during tempering. Framing should conceal them.
  - **Bow**: slight warping (an S shape or long arch) from heat treatment.
  - **Laminated glass**: two or more glass layers bonded with a **polyvinyl butyral (PVB)** interlayer that holds broken pieces together.
  - **Polycarbonate**: a dimensionally stable thermoplastic resin with high impact strength, laminated in for ballistic or impact resistance.
- **Main points**:
  - **Tempered disadvantages**: it must be ordered to exact size and cannot be cut, drilled or deeply etched after tempering. It can also show distortion. When butt-joining large panes frameless, orient the bows the same way.
  - Glass is tempered either horizontally or hanging vertically from tongs.
    - For glass up to 3/8 in (9.5 mm), the tong-mark centerline is at most 1/2 in (12.7 mm) from the edge.
    - For glass over 3/8 in, it is at most 3/4 in (19 mm) from the edge.
  - Bowing is the most noticeable distortion. It depends on thickness and the longest edge.
  - **Laminated glass**:
    - Can use float, tempered or heat-strengthened glass. More polycarbonate layers with PVB or polyurethane interlayers add ballistic resistance.
    - Laminated tempered glass or polycarbonate is used for very strong glazing. Float or tempered laminated glass is used for acoustics, and it can be bullet resistant.
    - Qualifies as safety glazing and can be cut in the field. Impact resistance is low unless tempered or heat-strengthened glass is used.
    - Thicknesses run 13/64 to 3 in (5.2–76 mm). Interior work typically uses 9/32 to 3/4 in (7.1–19 mm), from two plies of 1/8 in to two plies of 3/8 in.
    - Maximum sheet is 84 × 160 in (2134 × 4064 mm). The PVB interlayer is 0.015–0.090 in (0.381–2.286 mm) or more.
    - See Ch 11 (acoustics) and Ch 14 (security glazing).
  - **Table 5.1 Selected Bow Tolerances for Tempered Glass** (per ASTM C1048; tolerance shrinks as glass gets thicker and grows with edge length). Samples:
    - 20–35 in edge: 0.16 in (1/8 glass), 0.12 (1/4), 0.08 (3/8), 0.08 (1/2–7/8)

### Ch 5, pp 5-4 to 5-5 — Wired, Figured/Patterned, Mirrored, Two-Way Mirrors; Decorative Glass: Beveled, Etched, Stained, Hand-Blown, Cathedral
(The photo is rotated, but the text was readable.)
- **Key terms**:
  - **Wired glass**: glass with a wire mesh (hexagonal or square) embedded in the middle, smooth or patterned. It is used mainly in fire-rated openings.
  - **Figured/patterned glass**: molten glass passed through patterned rollers on one or both sides. It diffuses vision without fully obscuring it.
  - **Mirrored glass**: glass with a thin metal or metallic oxide film on one surface and a protective backing.
  - **Transparent (two-way) mirror**: a mirror that gives vision from one side and a mirror from the other. It is used for undetected observation (detention, psychiatric, casinos).
  - **Decorative glass**: glass usually made and installed by artists or craftspersons as one-of-a-kind work.
  - **Beveled glass**: glass with a decorative edge, usually a sloped polished surface, or multifaceted, bull-nose or curved profiles.
  - **Etched glass**: glass with part of the surface removed by acid or sandblasting. Uniform etching is **frosted**, and very deep etching is **carved glass**.
  - **Stained glass**: glass colored with metal oxides during manufacture, set in small pieces in H-shaped **cames** (lead or zinc) within a heavier frame.
  - **Hand-blown glass**: flat glass made by blowing a cylinder, cutting it and unrolling it. Sheets are small with unique irregularities.
  - **Flashed glass**: hand-blown glass with a clear layer plus a thin colored layer.
  - **Crackle glass**: hot glass dipped in water to create a webbed pattern.
  - **Seedy glass**: glass blown before it is fully refined, leaving small light-scattering particles.
  - **Cathedral glass**: machine-rolled glass textured on one side and smooth on the other. It is transparent.
- **Main points**:
  - **Wired glass**:
    - Must meet NFPA 80 and be UL-classified to serve as fire-protection-rated glazing.
    - **It can't be tempered, so it doesn't qualify as safety glazing** in hazardous locations.
    - Fire-rated wired glass openings are limited to **1296 in² (0.836 m²)** with a maximum dimension of **54 in (1372 mm)**.
  - **Mirrors**:
    - Made from float glass, or tempered glass where safety glazing is required.
    - Install with mechanical methods, not mastic alone: J-moldings, frames, clips, or drilled holes with screws or rosettes. Use a rubber sleeve so the fastener doesn't touch the glass.
  - **Two-way mirrors**: the observed room needs at least **5× the light** of the observation room (10× is better). The coated side faces the observed (lighter) room.
  - **Etching minimum glass thickness**:
    - Light etching or frosting: 1/8 in (3 mm); works on float, tempered, laminated and insulated glass.
    - Slightly deeper etching: 1/4 in (6 mm).
    - Medium etching or carving: 3/8 in (9 mm).
    - Heavy carving: 1/2 in (12 mm) or more.
  - Deep etching or carving must be done **before** tempering. Light etching can be done after.
  - Stained glass was traditionally religious and is now also used in residential and commercial work. In ceilings or damage-prone spots, cover it with clear tempered or laminated glass (or plastic if code allows).
  - **Sidebar, "Lighting Etched Glass"**: the frosted part itself must be lit. Use directional light from above on the etched side, or edge lighting (fluorescent) centered on the glass thickness. **Edge lighting fails beyond about 18 in (457 mm)** from the source.

### Ch 5, pp 5-6 to 5-7 — Opalescent, Painted, Dichroic, Cast, Kiln-Formed Glass; Special Glazing [088100]: Fire-Rated Glazing
(The photo is rotated, but the text was readable.)
- **Key terms**:
  - **Opalescent glass**: machine-formed glass with one or more colors mixed in. It is translucent and marbleized.
  - **Painted glass**: vitreous paint fired onto clear glass in a kiln, and it can be fired several times. It allows smaller, more intricate designs than stained glass.
  - **Dichroic glass**: glass coated by vacuum thin-film deposition with a stack of metal oxide layers 3–5 millionths of an inch thick. It reflects one color and transmits others depending on viewing angle.
  - **Cast glass**: molten glass poured into a sand mold made from a positive model. It is thick, suits interior or exterior use, and usually comes in small pieces.
  - **Kiln-formed glass**: flat glass heated until it sags over a mold, giving sculptural relief on one side and a smooth other side.
  - **Fire-rated glazing**: five types, namely **wired glass, clear ceramic, gel-filled glass, tempered glass, and some glass block** (Ch 1).
  - **Clear ceramic**: not actually glass. It is a clear ceramic with low thermal expansion and higher impact resistance than wired glass.
  - **Gel-filled glass (transparent wall panel / fire-resistance-rated glazing)**: two or three layers of tempered glass with a clear polymer gel between them. The gel turns opaque in fire and insulates against heat.
- **Main points**:
  - **Dichroic glass**: a single stack gives rainbow-like colors that shift with angle but has no red or green. A two-stack design gives all colors, and custom colors are possible.
  - **Cast glass**: usually custom-made. Some manufacturers mass-produce cast glass wall and floor tiles.
  - **Kiln-formed glass**:
    - 1/4–1/2 in (6–12 mm) thick, pieces up to 4 × 8 ft (1200 × 2400 mm).
    - Can be tempered, drilled and notched.
  - Special glazing is one of the fastest-growing areas of building product innovation.
  - **Wired glass (fire-rated)**:
    - Once the only glass permitted in fire-rated assemblies.
    - 1/4 in (6 mm) thick in a steel frame gives a **45-min rating** up to **1296 in² (9 ft², 0.84 m²)**, with no dimension over **54 in**.
    - In hazardous locations it must meet **16 CFR 1201** (formerly the less strict ANSI Z97.1). That requires lamination or a surface-applied film, so wired glass is now used less.
  - **Clear ceramic** (one manufacturer's product):
    - **1-hour rating** up to 1296 in², **3-hour rating** up to 100 in² (0.0645 m²).
    - Only 3/16 in (4.8 mm) thick, sizes up to 48 × 96 in, polished or patterned.
    - Some ceramic doesn't meet safety glazing requirements. Laminated versions are rated up to 2 hours and meet both ANSI Z97.1 and 16 CFR 1201.
  - **Gel-filled glass**:
    - Tested per **ASTM E119** (Ch 19). It resists hose-stream thermal shock, resists heat transfer, and passes safety impact tests.
    - Expensive, rated **up to 2 hours**, and must be installed exactly per the manufacturer. Maximum sizes are in Table 5.2.
  - **Framing for fire-resistance-rated glazing**: it must be tested with the glazing to ASTM E119. It is larger than standard wood, aluminum or hollow metal framing (Fig 5.1) and is a proprietary coordinated system, so consult the manufacturer.
  - **Sidebar, "Building Code Requirements for Fire-Rated Glazed Opening Assemblies"**:
    - Fire barriers must (1) restrict flame, (2) limit smoke spread, and (3) retard heat transmission.
    - Most glazing handles the first two but doesn't insulate. Radiant heat alone can ignite nearby combustibles without flame passing through.
    - **1-hour corridor partitions need 45-min opening protection**, but their doors may be **20-min**.
    - Other 1-hour partitions (occupancy separations, 1-hour stairways) need **60-min** opening protection. See Ch 19.

### Ch 5, pp 5-10 to 5-11 — Electrochromic Types; Other Special Glazing; Framed Glazing [08 81 00]; Wood/Steel Framing
- **Key terms**:
  - **Electrochromic glazing (specific type)**: Uses an inorganic ceramic thin-film coating. It tints from clear to heavily darkened but never becomes opaque, so it can't be used for privacy.
  - **SPD glazing**: Light-absorbing particles suspended in a liquid film between two conductive layers. It ranges from clear to fully opaque and can be varied continuously.
  - **Polymer-dispersed liquid crystal glazing**: A polymer film between two panes that switches from transparent to cloudy white. It gives privacy but still passes a lot of light, so it can't be used to control exterior light.
  - **Low-iron glass**: Glass with reduced iron content, so it lacks the usual green tint and is perfectly clear.
  - **Antireflective glass**: Float glass with a thin interference coating that cuts reflection.
  - **LED embedded glazing**: Laminated glass with LEDs encapsulated inside, powered through a clear conductive coating.
  - **Photochromic glass**: Changes tint in response to light level.
  - **Thermochromic glass**: Changes tint in response to temperature.
  - **Framed glazing**: Glass set in wood, steel or aluminum frames. It's the most common type of interior glazing.
  - **Setting blocks**: Small blocks the glass rests on so it never touches the frame directly.
- **Main points**:
  - **Electrochromic glazing (specific type)**: Controls light, UV and solar heat gain. Tinting isn't just on/off; a rheostat can control it.
  - **Cost and savings**: All electrochromic types are very expensive. Electrochromic and SPD can save 20–30% of energy.
  - **Low-iron glass**: High solar transmission and less color distortion. Use it where absolute clarity is needed or glass edges are visible. Can be tempered or laminated.
  - **Antireflective glass**: Reflection drops from about 8% (float glass) to under 1%. Good for show windows, display cases and storefronts. Comes single, tempered, laminated or insulated.
  - **LED embedded glazing**: 10 mm or 14 mm thick (about 3/8 or 9/16 in), in sizes up to 1300 × 2500 mm (51 × 98 in).
  - **Other special glass**: Some products shield against electromagnetic interference (formerly with wire mesh, now available clear).
  - **Effect of exterior glass on interiors**: The window glass type changes how much UV fading interior finishes suffer. Specify fade-resistant materials.
  - **Framed glazing**: Interior glass doesn't have to resist wind or be weather-sealed, so the size of the framing members is less critical.
  - **Wood framing (Figs 5.2–5.4)**:
    - Leave at least 1/4 in (6 mm) of shim space on all sides.
    - One stop must be removable.
    - Put two setting blocks at the quarter points of the sill.
    - Fill the gap between the glass and the stops with silicone sealant or a preformed neoprene glazing tape.
  - **Glass thickness**:
    - 1/4 in (6 mm) is standard for most interior glazing.
    - Small openings such as pass-throughs can use double-strength 1/8 in or 3/16 in (3–5 mm).
    - Large openings need thicker glass.
    - Hazardous locations need tempered or laminated glass.
  - **Steel framing**: Also called hollow-metal framing, like door framing.

### Ch 5, pp 5-20 to 5-21 — All-Glass System (Fig 5.13); Safety Glazing [08 81 00]; Coordination
- **Key terms**:
  - **Safety glazing**: Glass required in hazardous locations (places likely to be hit by people). Tempered and laminated glass qualify.
  - **Hazardous locations**: Places where glass is subject to human impact, such as doors, shower and bath enclosures, and certain wall locations.
  - **Category I / Category II**: Safety glazing classes under 16 CFR 1201. Category I is impact-tested at 150 ft-lbf (203 N·m) and Category II at 400 ft-lbf (542 N·m).
  - **16 CFR 1201**: The federal *Safety Standard for Architectural Glazing Materials*.
  - **ANSI Z97.1**: An alternative test with Class A and Class B. It is allowed only for glazing not used in doors or in enclosures for hot tubs, whirlpools, saunas, steam rooms, bathtubs and showers.
- **Main points**:
  - **Hazardous locations (IBC Sec 2406)**:
    - Swinging doors, including storm doors (jalousies excepted).
    - Fixed and sliding panels of sliding doors, and panels in sliding and bifold closet doors.
    - Unframed swinging doors.
    - Walls, doors and enclosures containing or facing hot tubs, spas, whirlpools, saunas, steam rooms, bathtubs, showers or pools, where the bottom exposed edge is less than 60 in (1524 mm) above the standing surface and the glass is within 60 in of the water's edge horizontally.
    - Guards and railings, including structural baluster and nonstructural infill panels, regardless of area or height.
    - Panels within 36 in (914 mm) of a walking surface (with conditions and exceptions; see Fig 5.14).
    - Panels next to a door whose nearest exposed edge is within 24 in (610 mm) of the door.
  - **Which category is required**:
    - Hazardous locations over 9 ft² (0.836 m²) need Category II.
    - Panels under 9 ft² need only Category I, except that doors, pool and sauna enclosures, and sliding patio doors need Category II.
    - Fixed panels under 9 ft² next to a walking surface have no requirement.
  - **National Building Code of Canada**: Less prescriptive. It requires tempered or laminated glass in doors, shower enclosures, and sidelights wider than 500 mm (1 ft 7-5/8 in).
  - **Fig 5.14 notes**:
    - Any glass door must be tempered.
    - Glass under 9 ft² in doors or within 24 in of doors must be at least Category I.
    - Other callouts: dimensions of 18 in (457 mm), 24 in (610 mm) and 60 in (1524 mm).
    - A crash bar must withstand a horizontal load of 50 lbf/ft (730 N/m); callouts show 1-1/2 in (38 mm) minimum and 34–38 in (864–965 mm).
  - **Coordination checklist**:
    - Verify large sheets can be transported into the building.
    - Specify horizontally tempered glass to avoid visible tong marks in frameless glazing.
    - Show which way the pattern faces on patterned or etched glass.
    - Match the thickness of frameless glass to an adjacent glass door.
    - Add markings on full-height glass so people don't walk into it.
    - Furniture or planters can serve as barriers in front of glass.
  - **Fig 5.13, all-glass system**: Shows structural support, glass mullion, patch fitting and silicone sealant in plan. The section shows a glass support at the base that varies with loading.

### Ch 16, pp 16-12 to 16-13 — Halogen/Low-Voltage (cont.); Fluorescent; Emergency Lighting; HID
- **Key terms**:
  - **Low-voltage lamps**: Incandescent lamps (standard or halogen) that run at less than 120 V, usually 12 V. They have thicker filaments and need a transformer.
  - **Fluorescent lamp**: A more efficient source than incandescent, used where a linear source is needed. It needs a ballast.
  - **T8 / T5**: Fluorescent tubes 1 in and 5/8 in in diameter, replacing the T12.
  - **EPAct (Energy Policy Act of 1992)**: The law that ended manufacture of many older T12 lamps.
  - **Compact fluorescent (CF) lamp**: A T4 or T5 tube bent into a U-shape with pins at one end.
  - **GU24 lamp**: A newer fluorescent lamp with a two-pin base that can't accept other lamp types. It replaces the Edison screw base.
  - **Ballast**: A device that supplies the proper starting and operating voltage to fluorescent and HID lamps.
  - **HID (high-intensity discharge) lamp**: Produces light by passing current through a gas or vapor under high pressure.
  - **Mercury vapor lamp**: An HID lamp whose arc runs through mercury vapor, giving blue-green light. It lasts long but renders color poorly.
  - **Metal halide lamp**: A mercury-type lamp with metal halides added, for better efficacy and color but shorter life.
  - **Emergency lighting**: Code-required lighting for evacuating a building during a power failure.
- **Main points**:
  - **Halogen**:
    - More efficacy and bluer, whiter light than standard incandescent.
    - Runs at high temperature and pressure and can shatter explosively, so it must be enclosed in an outer bulb or covered by glass or a screen.
  - **Low-voltage lamps**:
    - Compact, with good beam control; used in small luminaires or where a narrow beam is needed.
    - Efficient for lighting small objects from a distance.
    - Downside: the transformer makes the luminaire bulkier and raises its initial cost.
  - **Incandescent pros and cons**:
    - Pros: inexpensive, compact, easy to dim, can be restarted often without shortening life, warm color, easy to control with reflectors and lenses.
    - Cons: low efficacy, short life, high heat output.
  - **Compact fluorescent**:
    - About 10,000-hour life.
    - Two, three or four bends.
    - Some have a built-in ballast and a medium screw base to fit older fixtures.
  - **GU24**:
    - Color rendering index (CRI) of 80 or higher.
    - 40–70 lm/W.
    - 10,000–30,000-hour life.
    - Runs on 120 V or 277 V.
    - Energy Star residential fixtures can't use the Edison base.
  - **Ballast space**: Details must leave room for the ballast, either near the lamp or remote.
  - **HID types**: Mercury vapor, metal halide, and high-pressure sodium (HPS).
  - **Metal halide**: Offers the best overall combination of HID features. Its main drawback is a large shift in apparent color temperature over the lamp's life.
  - **Emergency lighting codes**: The IBC, NEC, *Life Safety Code* and Canadian codes govern it.
  - **Where emergency lighting is required**:
    - Exit stairs and corridors.
    - Places of assembly.
    - Educational and hazardous occupancies.
    - Occupancies whose load exceeds a set number.
  - **Emergency lighting levels and exit signs**:
    - Minimum 1 fc (10.8 lux) at the floor.
    - An exit sign at each exit door and at each door leading to an exitway.
    - Directional signs at corridor intersections and where the exit direction changes.
    - Luminous egress path markings in some high-rise occupancies (see Ch 20).
  - **Remodeling**: Remodels must include proper connection and relocation of emergency circuits and exit signs.

### Ch 16, pp 16-14 to 16-15 — Metal Halide/CMH/HPS; LEDs; Neon & Cold-Cathode; Lighting Systems
- **Key terms**:
  - **Ceramic metal halide (CMH) lamp**: Metal halide with a ceramic arc tube instead of quartz. It burns hotter, giving better color and higher efficacy.
  - **High-pressure sodium (HPS) lamp**: An HID lamp whose arc passes through hot sodium vapor in a ceramic tube. It's very efficient but gives yellow light.
  - **LED (light-emitting diode)**: A solid-state semiconductor device that emits light. OLEDs and light-emitting polymers are part of the same solid-state family.
  - **Neon lamp**: Gas-filled tubing that can be bent into almost any shape. Different gases give different colors.
  - **Cold-cathode lamp**: Similar to neon but larger (about 1 in / 25 mm in diameter) and more efficient. Comes in several whites and colors.
  - **Troffer**: A recessed fluorescent fixture, common in commercial ceilings.
  - **Cove lighting**: Concealed lighting in a cove that washes a ceiling or wall.
- **Main points**:
  - **Metal halide outer bulbs**:
    - Three kinds: clear, phosphor-coated and diffuse.
    - Use clear bulbs where optical control is needed.
    - Use coated or diffuse bulbs for better color, as in recessed downlights with low ceilings.
  - **CMH**:
    - Better color consistency over life than older metal halide.
    - Drawbacks: higher cost, hard to dim, needs a ballast.
    - Used in high-ceiling spaces and in retail, where point-source control and excellent color matter.
  - **HPS**:
    - 80–140 lm/W, among the most efficient lamps available.
    - Life is about 10,000 hours for improved-color lamps and up to 24,000 hours for others.
  - **HID in interiors**: Limited to general lighting of large spaces such as gyms, parking garages and industrial areas.
  - **LED advantages**:
    - Brightness, long life (over 50,000 hours) and low power use.
    - Produce no heat and can be controlled digitally.
    - Many colors.
  - **LED drawbacks**: Higher cost and, at the time of writing, relatively lower efficacy than traditional sources. LED replacements now exist for A, R, PAR and MR lamps and for fluorescent tubes.
  - **Neon and cold-cathode**: Both need transformers and small metal mounting brackets.
  - **Recessed lighting and clearance**:
    - Recessed lighting needs clear space above the ceiling.
    - Troffers are usually no problem.
    - Large incandescent downlights may force HVAC ducts, conduit or plumbing to be moved.
    - Avoid relocating fixtures by choosing low-clearance models.
    - Check clearances on the architectural and mechanical drawings and above the actual ceiling.
  - **Cove lighting design**:
    - The configuration depends on the supporting construction, luminaire size, sight lines, photometrics and design intent.
    - Use fixtures built specifically for coves.
    - Leave clearance for relamping, especially long fluorescent tubes.
  - **Fig 16.7, types of cove lighting**:
    - (a)–(c): strip fluorescent. Callouts: the lamp must stay out of the line of sight from the opposite side of the room, and must be far enough from the surface for even light without hot spots.
    - (d): wall cove with a standard strip or a wall-wash luminaire with reflector, and an optional lens.

### Ch 16, pp 16-16 to 16-17 — Control Devices [26 51 00]
- **Key terms**:
  - **Three-way / four-way switches**: Switches that control the same lights from two or three locations.
  - **Multilevel lighting control**: Controls that reduce lighting power in steps while keeping illuminance reasonably even.
  - **Programmable time-of-day controllers**: Controls that turn lights off at scheduled times. They range from single switch-box units to building management systems, and include override features.
  - **Occupant sensors**: Sensors that turn lights on or off based on occupancy. They can be passive infrared, ultrasonic or both, and manual switches can override them.
  - **Daylight compensation controls**: Photocells that dim or switch lights as daylight changes.
  - **ANSI/ASHRAE/IES Standard 90.1**: *Energy Standard for Buildings Except Low-Rise Residential Buildings*, which sets lighting control requirements.
- **Main points**:
  - **Switching decisions** depend on:
    - The space's function.
    - How much individual control is needed.
    - Where switches go (usually at the entry door).
    - Energy conservation.
    - The maximum load on any one circuit.
  - **Dimmers and circuits**:
    - Dimmed lights go on their own circuit. Both incandescent and fluorescent lights can be dimmed, but fluorescent dimmers cost more and need special fixtures.
    - In commercial buildings, incandescent usually runs on 120 V circuits and fluorescent on 277 V, which is more efficient.
  - **Two-level switching**: For example, a four-lamp fluorescent fixture on two switches gives two light levels. Dimmers alone have proven ineffective for saving energy, so automatic controls are required.
  - **Daylight compensation**:
    - Feasibility depends on latitude, climate, surroundings, building orientation, window exposure, and glazing type and size.
    - The whole perimeter zone using it must get at least half its light from daylight for several hours a day.
    - Well designed, it can cut perimeter energy use by up to 60%.
  - **Standard 90.1 automatic shutoff** applies to buildings over 5000 ft² (464 m²), using one of three methods:
    1. Time-of-day controls, for areas no larger than 25,000 ft² (2320 m²) and no more than one floor.
    2. Occupant sensors that turn lights off within 30 minutes of the space emptying.
    3. A signal from another control that shows the area is unoccupied.
  - **Exceptions to automatic shutoff**: 24-hour operations, patient care areas, and places where automatic shutoff would endanger safety or security.
  - **Per-space controls under 90.1**:
    - Each space enclosed by ceiling-height partitions needs at least one control device.
    - Classrooms, conference rooms, and lunch and break rooms need occupant sensors that shut lights off within 30 minutes.
    - Each device may control at most 2500 ft² (232 m²) in spaces up to 10,000 ft² (930 m²), and at most 10,000 ft² in larger spaces.
    - Each device must be able to override the scheduled shutoff for up to 4 hours.
    - Exterior lighting also needs controls (photosensor or astronomical time switch), except lighting needed for safety or security.
  - **Separate control is required for**:
    - Display and accent lighting.
    - Hotel and motel rooms: a master device at the entry controlling all permanent luminaires and switched receptacles.
    - Supplemental task lighting, including under-cabinet lights: a built-in or wall-mounted control.
    - Nonvisual lighting, such as food warming.
    - Lighting equipment for sale or demonstration.
  - **Circuit limits**: The number of switches depends on electrical load limits set by the engineer or contractor. Codes cap the wattage per circuit, so large, heavily lit spaces need several circuits.

### Ch 16, pp 16-18 to 16-19 — Custom Detailing for Lighting; Daylighting
- **Key terms**:
  - **UL (Underwriters Laboratories)**: The testing agency whose approved components let a custom fixture be installed without further approval.
  - **Daylighting**: Using natural light to supplement or replace electric light.
  - **Clerestory**: A vertical band of glazing set between two different roof levels to admit light.
  - **Effective daylighting depth**: About 2.5 × h, where h is the height from the floor to the top of the window.
  - **Light shelf**: A horizontal surface above eye level that reflects direct daylight onto the ceiling. It shades the lower window and spreads light deeper into the room.
- **Main points**:
  - **Custom fixtures**:
    - The designer sets the parameters and a qualified lighting designer develops the details.
    - Use UL-approved components to avoid further approvals.
    - A fully custom luminaire may need long, expensive testing before code officials accept it.
  - **Energy share of lighting**: Electric lighting and the cooling it requires account for 30–40% of a commercial building's energy use, and sometimes as much as 50%.
  - **Daylighting benefits**: Saves energy, reduces reliance on nonrenewable power plants, and helps occupants physically and psychologically.
  - **Photocell savings**: Photocell dimming can cut perimeter lighting energy by 50–60%.
  - **Designer strategies**:
    - Use open planning and keep window areas available for views.
    - Use low partitions and extensive interior glazing.
    - Choose window coverings that maximize daylight.
    - Use highly reflective surfaces.
    - Coordinate electric lighting with daylight.
    - In spaces with clerestories, keep partitions and office systems low.
  - **Daylighting variables (Fig 16.8)**:
    - Orientation of the facades.
    - Sky brightness (solar altitude, clouds, time of day).
    - Glass area, window head height, and glass transmittance.
    - Room and ground reflectance.
    - Obstructions such as overhangs and trees.
  - **Daylighting problems**:
    - Unwanted heat gain or loss.
    - Glare.
    - Unbalanced lighting when side light is too strong.
    - No energy saved unless electric lights are switched off; automatic switching solves this.
  - **Depth limit**: Daylight falls off rapidly beyond about 2.5h, which suggests where enclosed rooms should go.
  - **South sides**: Glare and heat gain from direct sun are the main problems.
  - **Light shelves (Fig 16.9)**:
    - Fig 16.9 compares no shelf, an exterior shelf only, and an exterior-plus-interior shelf.
    - Shelves work best partly outside and partly inside the glass.
    - The interior designer usually doesn't control whether one is used. In older buildings with large, high windows, the designer may add interior shelves.
    - Prefabricated shelves attach to the window mullions.

### Ch 16, pp 16-20 to 16-21 — Plumbing System Requirements [22 10 00]; Locating Plumbing Fixtures
- **Key terms**:
  - **Trap**: A water-holding fitting at almost every fixture. The water forms a seal that keeps sewer gas out of the building.
  - **Vent**: A pipe open to outside air. It lets sewer gases escape and equalizes pressure so draining water doesn't siphon traps dry.
  - **Fixture branch line**: A pipe carrying waste from a trap to a vertical stack.
  - **Soil stack**: A vertical stack that carries human waste from toilets.
  - **Waste stack**: A vertical stack that carries only non-human waste.
  - **Stack vent**: The part of a soil or waste stack above the highest fixture, extending through the roof.
  - **Vent stack**: A separate vertical vent pipe in multistory buildings. It extends through the roof or joins the stack vent above the highest fixture.
  - **Cleanout**: An access fitting for clearing the drain line (shown in Fig 16.10).
  - **Island fixture venting**: One of three ways to vent island sinks, which have no wall behind them.
- **Main points**:
  - **Two major components**: Hot and cold water supply, and drainage.
  - **Water supply**:
    - Water is under pressure and the pipes are small, so they fit easily in walls and ceilings.
    - Fixtures can be far from the source, but closer is cheaper.
  - **Drainage**: Works by gravity, so pipes must slope downward and vents are required. This makes it harder to plan than supply.
  - **Where traps are omitted**:
    - Fixtures with built-in traps, such as toilets.
    - Two or three adjacent fixtures sharing one trap, such as a double kitchen sink.
  - **Fig 16.10, drainage system components**: Stack vent, vent stack, trap, waste stack, soil stack, branch line, cleanout.
  - **Locating fixtures**: Place fixtures as close as possible to existing plumbing lines (horizontal lines or vertical risers).
  - **Drain slope**:
    - Minimum 1/4 in/ft (6 mm/300 mm).
    - 1/8 in/ft (3 mm/300 mm) is allowed for pipes larger than 3 in (76 mm).
  - **Horizontal run limit**: The pipe must fit within the floor or ceiling space. Its slope and size therefore limit how far a fixture can be from the riser (Fig 16.11).
  - **Vent distance**:
    - The vent must be reasonably close to the trap. Plumbing codes limit the trap-to-vent distance based on pipe size.
    - Table 16.2 gives the International Plumbing Code maximums.
    - Peninsula or island sinks with no wall behind them need special venting.
  - **Fig 16.11, planning for drain lines**: The drain depth is set by its slope and its highest point above the stack connection. Callouts include 1/4 in/ft (6 mm/300 mm), a suspended ceiling, a soil stack, and a distance of about 15 in (380 mm) that varies.

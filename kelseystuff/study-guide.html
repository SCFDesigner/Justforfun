How to use this: Read this page first (the highest-yield facts). Then skim each chapter's Key Terms and Main Points. The tables are there for number-heavy questions. Page references (e.g., p 1-12) point back to the book.

Top Must-Knows (if you only read one page)

Partitions (Ch 1)

  • Gypsum wallboard is the most common partition and ceiling material. The finished assembly is framing + board + trim + taped joints.
  • Type X is fire-rated board. Gypsum resists fire because its water of crystallization turns to steam and absorbs heat.
  • Most common commercial board is ⅝ in on metal studs at 24 in o.c. Use ½ in on studs at 16 in o.c.
  • Most common metal stud is 25 gage. A higher gage number means thinner steel. Most common stud depth is 2½ in, followed by 3⅝ in.
  • 1-hr partition (the most common rated wall) needs Type X, runs to the structure above, has all joints sealed, a slip joint at the top, and fire dampers in ducts.
  • 2-hr partitions separate occupancies and enclose stairs and elevator shafts.
  • STC (Sound Transmission Class): higher is better. At STC 25 normal speech is heard clearly. At STC 50 loud speech is not audible.
  • Levels of finish run 0–5. Level 1 is for plenums. Level 4 is the typical finish. Level 5 adds a skim coat, for gloss paint or harsh lighting.
  • Resilient channel separates the board from the studs to cut sound transmission.
  • Three-coat plaster goes scratch → brown → finish. Veneer plaster is a thin coat over "blueboard" gypsum lath.
  • Demountable/movable partitions have a higher first cost but a lower life-cycle cost. They carry no fire rating and can't hide plumbing.
  • Glass block is never loadbearing, is laid in stack bond, needs expansion joints at the top and sides, and its supports may deflect no more than L/600.

Ceilings (Ch 2)

  • Suspended acoustical (lay-in) is the most common commercial ceiling. It is cheap, fast to install, absorbs sound, and gives access to the plenum. Tiles absorb sound but don't block it.
  • Grid: main runners at 4 ft o.c. on hanger wires, with cross tees every 2 ft. A ceiling angle trims the perimeter.
  • Common tile sizes are 24×24 and 24×48 in. 20×60 tiles fit a 5-ft planning module.
  • The plenum is the space above the ceiling. If it is a return-air plenum, only noncombustible materials are allowed, and wiring must be in conduit or plenum-rated.
  • Seismic Design Category A–F is set by a structural engineer from soil class, risk category, and location. D/E/F are the strictest.
  • Risk Category IV covers essential facilities (hospitals with surgery or ER, fire and police stations). Its importance factor is 1.5.

Glazing (Ch 5)

  • Float glass is the most common, basic (annealed) glass. Tempered glass is about 4× stronger, breaks into small cubes, and can't be cut after tempering.
  • Laminated glass uses a PVB interlayer that holds broken pieces together. It counts as safety glass, can be cut, and helps with acoustics and security.
  • Wired glass is fire-rated but not safety glazing, because it can't be tempered.
  • Five fire-rated glazing types: wired, clear ceramic, gel-filled, tempered, and some glass block.
  • Fire-rated glazing label letters: W = wall, OH = openings, D = doors. H/NH = passes or fails the hose stream test. T/NT = passes or fails the temperature-rise test.
  • Safety glazing is required in hazardous locations: doors, tubs and showers, glass within 24 in of a door, and railings. It is tested to 16 CFR 1201. Category II (400 ft-lbf) is required for panels over 9 ft² and for all doors.
  • ¼ in is the standard interior glass thickness. Two-way mirrors need the observed room 5× brighter than the observation room.

Lighting (Ch 16)

  • Lamp families: incandescent/halogen, fluorescent (needs a ballast), HID (mercury vapor, metal halide, HPS), LED, and neon/cold-cathode.
  • Incandescent is cheap, dims easily, and has warm color, but it has low efficacy, a short life, and high heat.
  • LED lasts more than 50,000 hr, uses little power, and is digitally controllable.
  • HPS is very efficient (80–140 lm/W) but gives yellow light.
  • Emergency lighting must provide at least 1 fc at the floor, with exit signs at every exit door.
  • ASHRAE 90.1 requires automatic shutoff in buildings over 5000 ft². Occupant sensors must turn lights off within 30 min.
  • Daylighting reaches about 2.5 × window head height into a room. Light shelves bounce light deeper. Photocell dimming saves 50–60% of perimeter lighting energy.
  • Plumbing: traps block sewer gas and vents keep traps from siphoning. Drain lines slope at least ¼ in/ft.

Key Terms

Partition
An interior wall that divides space. It also supports doors and glazing and houses electrical and plumbing.
Gypsum wallboard ("gyp board," drywall)
A gypsum core between paper facings. It is the most common, most versatile partition material.
Mastic application
Gluing board directly to a substrate such as block or concrete instead of fastening it to framing.
Tapered edge
The standard long edge of a board. It is recessed so tape and compound sit flush.
Square edge
An edge used for base layers, under veneer plaster, or where looks don't matter.
Shaft wall (core) board
A 1 in thick, 2 ft wide board for enclosing shafts.
Type X (fire-rated) board
Board with mineral additives (vermiculite, glass fiber) in the core that improve fire resistance. It is required in rated walls.
Water of crystallization
Water bound in gypsum (about 50% by volume). In a fire it turns to steam and absorbs heat.
Water-resistant board
A tile backer for damp areas. It is not allowed in saunas, steam rooms, gang showers, on ceilings, or over vapor retarders.
Abuse-resistant board
Board that resists surface damage, penetration, and security breaches. Used in lobbies, hospital corridors, and gyms.
Sound-deadening board
Board that reduces sound. One type has a viscoelastic polymer core between gypsum layers.
Mold-resistant board
Board with a paper face replaced by an inorganic (fiberglass mat) facing, so mold has nothing to feed on.
Foil-backed board
Board with a foil vapor barrier, used when remodeling exterior walls and ceilings. The foil faces the framing.
Prefinished board
Board with vinyl already applied, used in demountable systems.
Dry / wet bending
Bending board onto closely spaced studs for curves. Wetting the board allows a tighter radius.
Gage
Steel stud thickness. A higher number means thinner steel; 25 ga is the most common.
Runners (tracks)
C-shaped floor and ceiling channels that hold the studs.
Dimpled stud
A stud whose rolled dimples work-harden the steel. It uses less steel, and its limiting heights are 15–25% higher.
Slip joint
A top-of-wall joint that lets the structure above deflect without cracking the board. It is required for rated partitions.
Top deflection track
A proprietary top runner with intumescent material that allows up to 1 in of deflection.
Intumescent
A material that swells and chars in heat, sealing against fire and smoke.
H-stud
A stud for area separation walls, which act as firebreaks between apartments or condos.
Shaft wall liner / shaft lining
A 2-hr enclosure around stairs, elevators, or dumbwaiters, with board on one side.
L/120, L/240, L/360
Deflection limits (L = length). L/120 is the code maximum. L/240 is recommended for brittle finishes.
Furring
Small framing members that give board something to attach to over other construction.
Hat channel
The most common metal furring, named for its hat-shaped profile.
Resilient channel
Furring with only one leg attached, which isolates the board and reduces sound transmission.
Z-furring
Z-shaped furring that gives more depth, for insulation or conduit.
Corner bead
L-shaped trim for all outside corners, covered with compound.
LC bead
Edge trim that goes on before the board and is finished with compound. Contractors also call it J or U bead.
L bead
Like LC bead but with no back flange, so it goes on after the board. Good for remodels.
U bead
Edge trim with no finishing. The metal stays visible, so it is the cheap option.
LK bead
Trim with a V edge that fits a kerf in adjacent wood trim.
Control joint
A joint in large expanses of board where movement is expected.
Reinforcing tape
Paper or fiberglass tape bedded in joint compound.
Textured coating
A finish such as "orange peel" that hides minor flaws.
Fire rating (1-, 2-, 3-, 4-hr)
How long an assembly stops fire, as measured by a lab test.
STC
Sound Transmission Class, a single-number rating of how well an assembly blocks sound. Higher is better.
Nonrated partition
A wall that runs from the floor to the suspended ceiling only. Common in commercial work.
Power-actuated fastener
A fastener driven by an explosive charge, used for the bottom runner on concrete.
Fire damper
A device in a duct that closes where the duct passes through a rated wall.
Chase wall
Two braced rows of studs spaced apart to hold plumbing or recessed items.
Soffit
A lowered section of ceiling, for example over cabinets, framed down from the ceiling.
High-attenuation partition
A double stud row that separates the two sides, reaching about STC 56–60.
Blocking
¾ in plywood or wood between studs that supports cabinets and wall-hung items.
Kickers
Braces from the top of a partition up to the structure. Used for heavy doors and cabinets.
Synthetic gypsum
A byproduct gypsum, mostly from flue gas desulfurization at power plants. It makes up about 28% of the supply.
Plaster
A cementitious material applied wet that sets hard. The binder is gypsum (typical interiors) or portland cement (wet areas).
Stucco
Exterior portland cement plaster.
Keene's cement
Dehydrated gypsum mixed with lime putty, used for extreme abrasion resistance.
Scagliola
Plaster finished to look like marble.
Sgraffito
Layered colored plaster with the top layer scratched away to reveal the colors below.
Marezzo
Imitation marble made from colored Keene's cement.
Fresco
Painting on wet plaster before it sets.
Veneer plaster
A very thin coat (1/16–⅛ in) over gypsum lath. It is fast, hard, and cheap, but not allowed in wet areas.
Gypsum lath ("blueboard")
Blue-faced board made to bond with veneer plaster.
Diamond mesh / flat rib / rib lath
Expanded metal laths. Diamond mesh bends into curves, flat rib is stiffer (for ceilings), and rib lath suits studless walls.
Scratch / brown / finish coat
The three plaster coats: scratch (~½ in, bond), brown (~¼ in, leveling), and finish (~⅛ in).
Lime (Venetian) plaster
Slaked lime putty plus crushed stone. It hardens by reabsorbing CO₂, is flexible and mold-resistant, and has no VOCs.
Security partition
High-strength plaster over perforated steel sheet, used in jails and vaults.
GRG (GFRG/FRG)
Glass-fiber-reinforced gypsum cast in molds, used for column covers, domes, and moldings. It is decorative only, never structural.
Masonry
Brick, concrete block, glass block, clay tile, terra cotta, and gypsum block.
CMU (concrete block)
A hollow cement-and-aggregate block, typically 8×8×16 in nominal (7⅝×7⅝×15⅝ in actual).
Nominal dimension
A size that includes the ⅜ in mortar joint and is based on a 4 in module.
Structural clay tile
A glazed, hard, easy-to-clean unit used in hospitals and food plants.
Stack bond
Units laid with joints aligned vertically, as glass block is.
Glass block
A hollow or solid glass unit, nominally 4 in thick ("thin" is 3⅛ in). It is never loadbearing.
L/600
The deflection limit for members supporting glass block.
Demountable partition
Prefinished components that can be taken down and reused but otherwise act like a permanent wall.
Progressive / nonprogressive
In a progressive system, panels must be removed in order (cheaper). In a nonprogressive system, any panel can be removed.
Movable partition
A wall not attached to the structure, often part of a furniture system. The term is often used interchangeably with demountable.
COM
Customer's Own Material, a finish the client supplies.
Class A flame spread
The best (most restrictive) flame-spread class.

Main Points

Gypsum wallboard basics (p 1-1 to 1-5)

  • Board widths are 4 ft and 4½ ft. Lengths are 8–14 ft, and installers use the longest practical length to reduce joints.
  • Thicknesses are ¼, ⅜, ½, ⅝, and ¾ in.
    • ⅝ in: commercial work.
    • ½ in: residential work.
    • ⅜ in: double layers and remodels.
    • ¼ in: curves and resurfacing.
    • ¾ in: a single layer can give 2 hr.
  • Board is usually hung horizontally, which means fewer joints, lower cost, and the board's strongest direction across the studs.
  • Board can go on studs, on furring, glued with mastic, or on ceiling framing. Mastic needs clean, smooth, flat surfaces.
  • Abuse-resistant board is made with core fibers, tough papers, Lexan or fiberglass backing, or veneer plaster. Impact ratings range from 60 to over 2100 ft-lbf.
  • Commercial showers use tile on cement board or a mortar bed, not gypsum.

Framing (p 1-5 to 1-11)

  • Wood (2×4, actually 1½×3½ in) is used in residential work, where walls can also carry loads. Metal is used in commercial work because it is noncombustible and light.
  • Studs are spaced 16 or 24 in o.c. 24 in is common for commercial nonloadbearing walls.
  • Metal stud depths are 1⅝, 2½, 3⅝, 4, and 6 in. 2½ in suits 8–10 ft ceilings. Use deeper studs for pipes, medicine cabinets, and pocket doors.
  • Heavier gages (20, 22) are for tall walls, door openings, and cabinets. Structural studs are 12–18 ga.
  • Assembly: fasten the runners first, then slip in the studs and screw or crimp them.
  • Furring comes as 1×2 wood (minimum), hat channel, resilient channel, or Z-furring.
  • Six standard trim shapes: corner bead, LC, L, U, LK, and control joint.
  • Finishing sequence: tape bedded in compound, then more coats feathered wider, sanding between coats. Screw heads get the same treatment.

Fire & sound (p 1-12 to 1-27)

  • Gypsum is naturally fire resistant, and its density makes it a good sound barrier.
  • Where each fire rating is used:
    • 1-hr: corridors, tenant separations, and house-to-garage walls.
    • 2-hr: occupancy separations and stair or elevator shafts.
    • 3–4 hr: some occupancy separations.
  • Rules for 1-hr partitions:
    • Use Type X board and run the wall to the structure above.
    • Seal all joints and penetrations, and put fire dampers in ducts.
    • Allow only one electrical box per stud space, on one side only, with a maximum of 16 in².
  • A 2-hr partition has two layers of Type X on each side, or a single layer of proprietary ¾ in board.
  • Cracking is worst in high-rises, where buildings deflect and sway. Use slip joints at slabs and relief joints at mullions and columns (½ in gap).
  • Ways to add sound control:
    • More layers of board.
    • Resilient channels.
    • Batt insulation.
    • A double stud row.
    • Sealing every penetration, since leaks ruin the rating.
  • Coordinate: floor deflection, horizontal sway, heavy loads (blocking), partition depth, and anchoring (kickers).
  • Code references: IBC Tables 721.1(2) and (3), the UL directory, and the GA Fire Resistance Design Manual.
  • The IBC requires water-resistant board behind tub and shower tile and on water-closet compartment walls.

Plaster (p 1-28 to 1-33)

  • Plaster's advantages over wallboard:
    • Single and double curves.
    • Ornate moldings.
    • Many textures.
    • Abrasion resistance.
    • Water resistance, with portland cement.
  • Ingredients are a binder, lime, water, and aggregate (sand, perlite, vermiculite). Use a portland cement binder in wet areas, because moisture damages gypsum plaster.
  • Three-coat plaster goes over metal lath. Two-coat plaster is mainly a base for tile.
  • Veneer plaster is faster, cheaper, and harder, but not allowed in wet areas. Two layers of rated lath give 2 hr.
  • Wet areas: use cement backer board, or full lath and plaster for very wet commercial spaces.
  • Rated partitions need fire-rated gypsum lath.
  • Lime plaster benefits:
    • Flexible, and small cracks self-heal.
    • Breathable.
    • High pH makes it a biocide that resists mold.
    • No VOCs, and it absorbs CO₂ as it sets.
    Over wallboard it needs a Level 4 or 5 finish.
  • Plaster control joints go where plaster meets structure, in runs over 30 ft, and at building expansion joints.
  • Plaster walls need door frames of at least 16 ga steel.
  • GRG tips:
    • Expose one side only.
    • Minimize the number of unique molds.
    • Use draft angles, not 90° corners.
    • Keep joints ≤ ⅜ in wide.
    • Extend curved pieces about 2 in into the flat board.

Masonry & glass block (p 1-33 to 1-37)

  • Interior masonry walls are nonloadbearing. A structural engineer must confirm the floor can carry them. Size openings to the 4 in module.
  • CMU is laid in running bond with joint reinforcement every 16 in vertically. Filling the cells with grout or sand improves sound control.
  • Glass block faces are 6×6, 8×8, 12×12, and 4×8 in. Thicknesses are 3⅞ in (standard) and 3⅛ in (thin, common indoors).
  • Code requirements for glass block:
    • Units at least 3 in thick.
    • Mortar joints ¼–⅜ in.
    • Expansion joints allowing at least ⅜ in of movement.
    • Stiffeners when a panel exceeds the code maximum.
  • Glass block usually has no fire rating. Some assemblies reach 45–90 min in openings up to 100 ft².
  • Curved glass block walls need stiffeners at every change of curve direction.

Demountable / movable (p 1-36 to 1-41)

  • Components: floor runner, ceiling runner, studs with clips, and prefinished vinyl gypsum panels (24 or 30 in wide, fitting 4 or 5 ft modules).
  • Operable partitions (Ch 3) open and close often, like doors. Demountable partitions stay put.
  • Pros:
    • Fast, clean reconfiguration with no wet compound.
    • Recycled and recyclable materials.
    • Single-source responsibility.
    • Tax advantages (treated as equipment).
    • Lower life-cycle cost.
    • Handles uneven floors.
  • Cons:
    • No fire rating.
    • Can't hide plumbing.
    • Limited acoustics (under STC 50 as installed) and limited security.
    • Limited cabinet support.
    • Higher first cost.
  • A spiked bottom track sits on carpet. The top track clips to the ceiling grid, so the space plan should align with the ceiling grid.
  • Most systems are Class A flame spread.

Chapter 2 — Ceilings

Suspended acoustical · Seismic · Gypsum wallboard · Plaster · Linear metal · Decorative · Integrated · Abuse-resistant · Metal & wood

Key Terms

Plenum
The space above a suspended ceiling, used for ducts, wiring, and other services.
Return air plenum
A plenum that also returns air to the HVAC system. Only noncombustible materials are allowed.
Plenum-rated wiring
Wiring allowed to run exposed in a plenum. All other wiring must be in metal conduit.
Suspended acoustical ceiling
Mineral, glass-fiber, or wood panels in a metal grid hung on wires. It is the most common commercial ceiling.
Lay-in system
Panels resting on an exposed T-grid. The most common type.
Tegular
Lay-in panels with rabbeted (stepped) edges that drop below the grid.
Concealed grid
A grid hidden by the tiles, usually 1×1 ft tiles. Beveled edges hide misalignment.
Main runners
Primary grid members, hung on wires at 4 ft o.c.
Cross tees
Secondary members that snap into the main runners every 2 ft.
Ceiling angle
L-shaped trim at the walls that supports edge tiles.
Concealed spline ceiling
Tiles with kerfed edges that slide onto a hidden grid.
Upward / downward accessible
Concealed-spline access methods. Upward (tiles pushed up onto Z-clips) is preferred, because the pull-down tool chips tiles.
Retention (hold-down) clips
Clips that keep tiles from lifting out. They are required for some rated ceilings.
Rated acoustical ceiling
Rated tiles on a rated grid with hold-down clips and expansion slots. It is only part of a rated floor/ceiling assembly.
Plenum sound barrier
Construction above a partition that stops sound from traveling over it through the plenum.
Seismic restraint
Bracing that keeps nonstructural items, such as ceilings, in place during earthquakes.
Seismic Design Category (A–F)
An IBC rating based on soil class + risk category + location. A is least restrictive and F is most. It is set by a structural engineer.
Risk (occupancy) category I–IV
How important it is for a building to survive an earthquake. IV covers essential facilities.
Importance factor
A multiplier per risk category: 1.00 for categories I and II, 1.50 for IV.
Soil class A–F
Soil ratings from A (hard rock) to F (special soil). Assume D when the soil is unknown.
CISCA
Ceilings & Interior Systems Construction Association, which publishes seismic ceiling guides (Zones 0–2 for Category C; Zones 3–4 for D/E/F).
ASCE 7
The standard for minimum design loads, including seismic provisions.
Compression struts (posts)
Rigid vertical braces from the grid to the structure. Required in D/E/F.
Splay wires
Diagonal bracing wires at 45° that restrain lateral movement.
Spacer bars
Bars that keep grid ends from spreading at the perimeter.
Furring channel (hat)
Light channel that gypsum board ceilings screw to.
Suspended GWB ceiling
1½ in steel channels at 4 ft plus furring, hung on wires, with board screwed on.
Ceiling radiation damper
A damper required where a diffuser penetrates a rated ceiling.
Access panel
A hinged steel door in a board ceiling (about 24 in square) for reaching valves and dampers.
Cloud
A separate panel suspended below the main ceiling.
Drapery pocket
A ceiling recess for drapery track or blinds.
Casing bead
Metal trim at plaster edges.
Perimeter relief (isolation) joint
A joint that separates a plaster ceiling from walls and structure.
Linear metal ceiling
Long prefinished aluminum strips clipped to carriers. The gaps let air and sound through.
Decorative suspended ceiling
Baffles, wood strips, open grids, mirrored panels, and similar, hung on wires or rods.
Integrated ceiling
One system combining tile, lights, HVAC, sprinklers, and partition attachment, on a 4 or 5 ft module. Good where layouts change often.
Abuse-resistant ceiling
A ceiling built to resist impact, vandalism, frequent access, or entry. Used in schools, hospitals, and prisons.
Metal & wood suspended
Aluminum or wood-veneer panels (often perforated) on a grid.
FSC-certified
Wood from a Forest Stewardship Council–certified forest, which counts as sustainable.
VOCs
Volatile organic compounds that offgas from materials. Aluminum has none.

Main Points

General & suspended acoustical (p 2-1 to 2-7)

  • Ceilings integrate lighting, partition attachment, air diffusers, and speakers, so their detailing is critical.
  • Three ceiling groups:
    • Suspended from the structure.
    • Attached directly to the structure.
    • Exposed (the structure is the finish).
  • Residential: board is screwed directly to the joists. Commercial: wire-suspended systems.
  • Advantages of suspended acoustical ceilings:
    • Low cost.
    • Fast installation.
    • Sound absorption (but not a sound barrier).
    • Flexibility.
    • Easy plenum access.
  • Tile sizes: 24×24 and 24×48 are most common. 20×60 fits a 5 ft planning module and is often used with demountable partitions.
  • Suspended ceilings are installed level within ⅛ in in 12 ft, which is more level than floors. That is why critical dimensions are measured from the ceiling down.
  • Specify the accessible percentage for concealed-spline ceilings. For example, 25% means a quarter of the tiles can be removed.
  • Concealed-spline joints show shadows under grazing light, so use ambient lighting.
  • Code: tiles are almost all Class A. Installation follows ASTM C635/C636 (E580 for seismic areas).
  • A ceiling alone can't stop fire from spreading between floors.
  • Plenum sound barrier options:
    • Cheapest: batt insulation on the tiles, 4 ft each side of the wall.
    • Better: run the partition to the structure, or hang a gypsum or lead barrier.

Seismic (p 2-6 to 2-13)

  • About half of the U.S. has seismic risk. Highest-risk areas:
    • California.
    • Parts of Utah, Idaho, and Montana.
    • The New Madrid area (the Arkansas–Tennessee–Missouri–Kentucky junction).
    • The Carolina border.
    • Alaska and Hawaii.
  • Risk categories:
    • I: low hazard (farm buildings, minor storage).
    • II: standard occupancies.
    • III: substantial hazard (assembly over 300 people, schools over 250, colleges over 500).
    • IV: essential facilities (hospitals with surgery or ER, fire and police stations, shelters, air traffic control).
  • No special seismic design is needed for:
    • Categories A and B.
    • Ceilings under 144 ft².
    • Gypsum board suspension systems.
    • Plaster and lath ceilings.
  • In D/E/F, partitions must be braced independently of the ceiling. In C, they may attach to the grid only if the ceiling can move laterally.
  • D/E/F grids attach on two adjacent walls only. Hanger wires go within 8 in of walls, and struts are placed 12 ft o.c.
  • Proprietary seismic clips must be lab-tested.

Coordination (p 2-12)

  • Coordinate recessed lights (clearance), ducts, sprinklers, speakers, smoke detectors, and drapery pockets.
  • No combustibles in a return plenum, not even fire-retardant wood.
  • Don't paint acoustic tile, because paint can kill its sound absorption. Clean or replace it instead.
  • Walls that stop at the ceiling need diagonal bracing to the structure when they carry heavy loads.

Gypsum wallboard ceilings (p 2-13 to 2-17)

  • Use ½ in board at 16 in o.c. and ⅝ in at 24 in o.c. Most commercial ceilings use 24 in spacing with ⅝ in board. Screws are preferred over nails.
  • Commercial assembly: 1½ in channels at 4 ft o.c. on wires, with furring at 16 or 24 in.
  • The whole suspension system must be noncombustible. Installation follows GA-216 and ASTM C840, C754, and C1007.
  • In rated assemblies, diffusers need radiation dampers and access doors must be rated. In most commercial buildings the floor structure, not the ceiling, provides the rating.
  • Board ceilings are smooth but hard to access, and access panels look bad, so limit them to areas without access needs.
  • Board can form stepped, coffered, vaulted, and arched shapes.
  • Combining board with acoustical tile gives the look of board plus the absorption and access of tile. Transitions use aluminum trim, bulkheads, or clouds.

Plaster ceilings (p 2-17 to 2-19)

  • Used for wet areas (public showers, steam rooms) and ornate remodels. Plaster curves in two directions but costs more.
  • Commercial assembly: 8 ga wires at 3 ft, 1½ in main runners at 4 ft, cross-furring, lath, and three coats.
  • Moldings are either run in place with a template or cast and attached.
  • Place relief joints at structure, penetrations, and dissimilar materials.
  • Control joints are required when the ceiling exceeds 50 ft (with relief joints) or 30 ft (without).

Specialty ceilings (p 2-19 to 2-22)

  • Linear metal: painted or anodized aluminum. The gaps allow sound absorption and return air.
  • Integrated: higher first cost, but it saves money when layouts change often, so run a life-cycle cost analysis.
  • Abuse-resistant ceilings, by level:
    • Light (commercial kitchens): faced tile.
    • Heavy: snap-in metal panels.
    • Maximum security: heavy steel planks with tamper-proof fasteners.
    Plaster and gypsum versions have poor acoustics.
  • Aluminum panel advantages:
    • Light (1–2 lb/ft²).
    • Can be custom shaped.
    • Recyclable.
    • Class A.
    • Unaffected by moisture.
    • No VOCs.
    • Good acoustics when perforated and backed with batts.
    Their drawback is a higher first cost.

Chapter 5 — Glazing

Common glass types · Decorative glass · Special & fire-rated glazing · Framed · Frameless · Safety glazing · Coordination

Key Terms

Glazing
Installing glass in framing. The word also means the glass itself.
Float glass
The most common glass, made by floating molten glass on molten tin and then annealing it. It replaced plate glass in the 1960s.
Annealed
Slowly cooled. Annealed float glass is the baseline strength.
Heat-strengthened glass
About 2× as strong as annealed. Mostly used for exterior windows. Not safety glass.
Tempered glass
Glass heated to about 1200°F and quenched. It is about 4× stronger, breaks into small cubes, and counts as safety glass. It can't be cut after tempering.
Tong marks
Small marks near an edge from tongs used during vertical tempering. Hide them in the frame, or specify horizontal tempering.
Bow
Warping from heat treatment. It is the most noticeable distortion in tempered glass.
Laminated glass
Glass layers bonded with a PVB (polyvinyl butyral) interlayer that holds pieces together when broken. It is safety glass, can be cut, and suits acoustic and security uses.
Polycarbonate
A tough thermoplastic laminated in for ballistic or impact resistance.
Wired glass
Glass with embedded wire mesh. It is fire-rated, but not safety glass because it can't be tempered.
Figured / patterned glass
Glass rolled with a pattern that obscures vision without fully blocking it.
Mirrored glass
Glass with a metallic film and a protective backing. Mount it mechanically, not with mastic alone.
Two-way (transparent) mirror
A mirror on one side and a window from the other. The observed room needs at least 5× more light (10× is better), and the coating faces the brighter room.
Beveled glass
Glass with a sloped, polished decorative edge.
Etched glass
Glass with part of the surface removed by acid or sandblasting. Uniform etching is frosted, and deep etching is carved.
Stained glass
Glass colored with metal oxides and set in lead or zinc cames (H-shaped strips).
Hand-blown glass
Glass blown as a cylinder, then cut and flattened. It has unique irregularities.
Flashed / crackle / seedy
Hand-blown variations: flashed has a thin colored layer, crackle has a webbed pattern from dipping in water, and seedy has small bubbles.
Cathedral glass
Machine-rolled glass, textured on one side and transparent.
Opalescent glass
Translucent, marbleized glass with multiple colors mixed in.
Painted glass
Vitreous paint fired onto glass. It allows finer detail than stained glass.
Dichroic glass
Glass with thin metal-oxide film layers. It reflects one color and transmits another, shifting with viewing angle.
Cast glass
Molten glass poured into a sand mold. It is thick and usually comes in small pieces.
Kiln-formed glass
Glass heated until it sags over a mold, giving relief on one side and a smooth other side.
Clear ceramic
A fire-rated clear ceramic, not actually glass. It is more impact-resistant than wired glass.
Gel-filled glass
Tempered layers with a gel between them that turns opaque and insulates in a fire. It is rated up to 2 hr and is expensive.
Fire-protective (tempered) glass
Rated for 30 min max, because it fails the hose stream test. It is ¼ in thick and still meets the 16 CFR 1201 impact standard.
W / OH / D
IBC labels for where fire-rated glazing may go: Wall, Opening (windows, sidelights), and Door. The rating in minutes follows.
H / NH, T / NT
H or NH means the glazing meets or fails the Hose stream test. T or NT means it meets or fails the Temperature-rise test. Example: "D-H-NT-45."
Bent glass
Flat glass heated and shaped into curves. Maximum size is about 4×8 ft.
Decorative laminated glass
Laminated glass with a colored film, fabric, rice paper, or mesh interlayer.
Electrochromic glazing
Glass that tints clear to dark when current is applied. It never becomes fully opaque, so it can't provide privacy, but it saves energy.
SPD (suspended-particle device)
Glass that varies continuously from clear to fully opaque.
PDLC (polymer-dispersed liquid crystal)
Glass that switches from clear to cloudy white. It gives privacy but doesn't control daylight.
Photochromic / thermochromic
Glass that tints in response to light or to temperature.
Low-iron glass
Glass with no green tint, for ultra-clear uses and visible edges.
Antireflective glass
Glass that cuts reflection from about 8% to under 1%. Used for display cases and storefronts.
LED-embedded glazing
Laminated glass with LEDs inside, powered through a clear conductive coating.
Framed glazing
Glass in wood, steel, or aluminum frames. The most common interior type.
Setting blocks
Small blocks the glass sits on, placed at the quarter points of the sill.
Removable stop
The frame piece that holds the glass in. Put it on the less important side of the wall.
Hollow-metal (steel) frame
A 16–18 ga bent steel frame, needed for fire-rated openings. Its stops show screws.
Aluminum frame
An extruded frame with sharp corners and snap-in stops (no exposed fasteners). It has no fire rating.
Face clearance / edge clearance / bite
The gap between the glass face and the stop, the gap at the glass edge, and how far the glass sits inside the frame.
Frameless glazing
Glazing with head and sill frames recessed and jambs hidden or eliminated, so the glass appears to float.
Butt joint / T-joint
A butt joint is two glass edges sealed with silicone. A T-joint is used where three pieces meet; one piece extends past the others.
All-glass system
Glass walls supported only at the top and bottom, braced by glass fins (mullions).
Patch fittings
Metal fittings that join glass pieces or doors without framing.
Shake
How much a glass panel flexes or vibrates. It affects the thickness choice.
Safety glazing
Tempered or laminated glass required in hazardous locations.
16 CFR 1201
The federal safety glazing standard. Category I is tested at 150 ft-lbf and Category II at 400 ft-lbf.
ANSI Z97.1
An alternate safety test (Classes A and B), not allowed for doors or tub, shower, or sauna enclosures.

Main Points

Common glass (p 5-1 to 5-3)

  • Float glass comes clear or tinted (bronze, gray, green). Thicknesses run ⅛ to ¾ in. Indoors it is used only where safety glass isn't required.
  • Tempered glass comes ⅛ to ⅞ in thick, and ¼ in is common indoors.
  • Tempered glass can't be cut, drilled, or deeply etched afterward, so order it to exact size.
  • When butting large frameless tempered panes, orient the bows the same way.
  • Tong marks sit within ½ in of the edge for glass up to ⅜ in thick, and within ¾ in for thicker glass.
  • Laminated glass:
    • Can be float, tempered, or heat-strengthened glass.
    • Can be cut in the field.
    • Good for acoustics and security (bullet-resistant with polycarbonate).
    • Low impact resistance unless tempered glass is used.
    • Interior sizes run 9/32 to ¾ in. Maximum sheet is 84×160 in.
  • Standards:
    • ANSI Z97.1 and 16 CFR 1201: safety glazing.
    • ASTM C1036: flat glass.
    • ASTM C1048: tempered and heat-strengthened glass.
    • NFPA 80: fire doors and windows.

Decorative glass (p 5-4 to 5-7)

  • Wired glass meets NFPA 80 and is UL-labeled. Openings are limited to 1296 in² (9 ft²) with a 54 in maximum dimension. It is not safety glazing.
  • Mount mirrors mechanically (J-moldings, clips, rosettes) with rubber sleeves so metal never touches the glass.
  • Minimum glass thickness for etching:
    • Light etching: ⅛ in.
    • Deeper etching: ¼ in.
    • Medium carving: ⅜ in.
    • Heavy carving: ½ in or more.
    Deep etching must be done before tempering.
  • Lighting etched glass: light the frosted part itself, from above or at the edge. Edge lighting fails beyond about 18 in.
  • Stained glass in ceilings or damage-prone spots should be protected with tempered or laminated glass.
  • Kiln-formed glass is ¼–½ in thick, up to 4×8 ft, and can be tempered.
  • Dichroic glass: a single film stack gives no red or green, and two stacks give all colors.

Fire-rated glazing (p 5-6 to 5-9)

  • Five types:
    • Wired glass.
    • Clear ceramic.
    • Gel-filled glass.
    • Tempered glass.
    • Some glass block.
  • Ratings by type:
    • Wired glass (¼ in, steel frame): 45 min.
    • Clear ceramic (only 3/16 in thick): 1 hr up to 1296 in², and 3 hr up to 100 in².
    • Gel-filled (tested to ASTM E119): up to 2 hr, and it blocks heat.
  • A fire barrier must stop flame, stop smoke, and limit heat transfer. Most glass does only the first two, and radiant heat can still ignite things nearby.
  • 1-hr corridor walls need 45-min glazing, and their doors may be 20-min. Other 1-hr walls need 60-min glazing.
  • Test standards: W uses ASTM E119, OH uses NFPA 257, and D uses NFPA 252.
  • Rated glazing needs rated framing tested with the glass, which is bulkier than standard framing.

Special glazing (p 5-9 to 5-11)

  • Three switchable types:
    • Electrochromic: tints but no privacy.
    • SPD: clear to fully opaque.
    • PDLC: privacy, but no light control.
    All are very expensive. Electrochromic and SPD save 20–30% of energy.
  • Low-iron glass is for absolute clarity. Antireflective glass is for displays.
  • Exterior glass type affects UV fading of interior finishes, so specify fade-resistant materials.

Framed glazing (p 5-10 to 5-15)

  • Interior glass has no wind load or weather sealing, so framing sizes are less critical.
  • Wood frames:
    • At least ¼ in of shim space.
    • One stop removable.
    • Two setting blocks at the sill quarter points.
    • Silicone or neoprene glazing tape.
  • Glass thickness:
    • ¼ in: the standard.
    • ⅛–3/16 in: small pass-throughs.
    • Thicker: large openings.
    • Tempered or laminated: hazardous locations.
  • Steel frames are for fire ratings and matching hollow-metal doors. They have rounded corners.
  • Aluminum frames have sharp corners, snap-in stops, and anodized finishes.

Frameless & all-glass (p 5-13 to 5-21)

  • Full-height glazing 8–10 ft tall needs at least ¼ in tempered, and ⅜ in is preferred. Use laminated glass for sound control.
  • Leave extra space at the head so the glass can be lifted in and swung upright.
  • Silicone sealant comes clear or black. Clear can show bubbles, so black often looks better.
  • Use a T-joint where three pieces meet, because sealant can't be installed well at a single point.
  • All-glass systems reach 30 ft when the weight rests on the base, or 75 ft when hung from clamps.
  • Blinds in a recessed blind pocket give conference rooms privacy.
  • A partial-height wall doesn't need safety glass if the sill is ≥ 18 in above the floor.

Safety glazing — hazardous locations (IBC 2406)

  • Hazardous locations:
    • Swinging, sliding, and bifold doors.
    • Unframed doors.
    • Tubs, showers, spas, saunas, steam rooms, and pools, where the glass is < 60 in above the standing surface and within 60 in of the water.
    • Guards and railings, always.
    • Panels near walking surfaces (the 18/36 in rules).
    • Glass within 24 in of a door.
  • Which category applies:
    • Panels over 9 ft² need Category II.
    • Panels under 9 ft² need Category I, except doors, pool and sauna enclosures, and sliding patio doors, which need Category II.
    • Every glass door must be tempered.
  • Canada requires tempered or laminated glass in doors, showers, and sidelights wider than 500 mm.

Coordination (p 5-21 to 5-22)

  • Make sure large sheets fit through the building to reach the space.
  • Specify horizontal tempering to avoid tong marks.
  • Note which way patterned or etched glass faces.
  • Match frameless glass thickness to adjacent glass doors.
  • Add markings on full-height glass so people don't walk into it. Furniture or planters can also act as barriers.
  • Put stops on the side of the wall where appearance matters least.
  • Leave at least 1½ in between exterior glass and blinds, with top and bottom gaps, so trapped heat can't crack the glass. Put HVAC outlets on the room side of the blinds.

Chapter 16 — Lighting (16-11 to 16-19) + Plumbing Start (16-20)

Mechanical & Electrical Systems Coordination · Conduit & communications (16-10) · Light sources · Lighting systems · Controls · Daylighting · Plumbing

Key Terms

EMT
Electrical metallic tubing, the most common steel conduit. It is light and bends easily.
IMC / RMC
Intermediate and rigid metal conduit, the heavier steel types.
NEC
National Electrical Code, which governs conduit sizing and bending.
Plenum-rated cable
Cable allowed without conduit in plenums. It costs more.
Incandescent lamp
A tungsten filament in a gas-filled bulb.
Tungsten-halogen (quartz)
An incandescent lamp with halogen gas. The halogen cycle redeposits tungsten on the filament, giving longer life, whiter light, and steady color.
Low-voltage lamp
A lamp that usually runs at 12 V and needs a transformer. It is compact and gives tight beam control.
MR lamp
A miniature reflector lamp (low-voltage) that allows small fixtures.
R / ER / PAR lamps
Reflector lamps. They need large clearances from combustibles.
Fluorescent lamp
An efficient linear source that needs a ballast.
T8 / T5
Fluorescent tubes 1 in (T8) and ⅝ in (T5) in diameter. They replaced the T12. The number is the diameter in eighths of an inch.
EPAct 1992
The Energy Policy Act, which ended production of many T12 lamps.
Compact fluorescent (CFL)
A bent T4 or T5 tube lasting about 10,000 hr. Some have a screw base to fit older fixtures.
GU24
A two-pin base that can't accept incandescent lamps. Its lamps have CRI ≥ 80, give 40–70 lm/W, and last 10–30k hr.
Ballast
A device that supplies starting and operating voltage to fluorescent and HID lamps. Leave room for it in details.
HID
High-intensity discharge lamps, which pass an arc through high-pressure gas: mercury vapor, metal halide, HPS.
Mercury vapor
An HID lamp with blue-green light, long life, and poor color.
Metal halide
An HID lamp with better color and efficacy but shorter life. It has the best mix of HID features, but its color shifts over its life.
Ceramic metal halide (CMH)
Metal halide with a ceramic arc tube, giving better color and consistency. Used in retail and high ceilings. It costs more and is hard to dim.
HPS
High-pressure sodium, with a yellow light. Very efficient (80–140 lm/W), lasting 10–24k hr.
LED
A light-emitting diode (solid-state). It lasts over 50,000 hr, uses little power, produces no heat in the beam, and can be digitally controlled.
OLED
An organic LED, part of the same solid-state family.
Neon
Gas-filled tubing bent into shapes. The gas sets the color, and it needs a transformer.
Cold-cathode
Like neon but larger (about 1 in diameter), more efficient, and available in whites.
Emergency lighting
Code-required lighting for evacuation during a power failure.
Troffer
A recessed fluorescent fixture in a ceiling grid.
Cove lighting
Hidden lamps in a cove that wash a ceiling or wall with light.
Three-way / four-way switch
Switches that control the same lights from two or three locations.
Multilevel (two-level) switching
Controls that reduce light in steps, for example half the lamps of a fixture on each switch.
Time-of-day controller
A control that turns lights off on a schedule, with an override.
Occupant sensor
A passive infrared or ultrasonic sensor that turns lights on and off based on occupancy.
Daylight compensation
Photocells that dim or switch lights as daylight changes.
ASHRAE/IES 90.1
The energy standard for buildings except low-rise residential. It sets lighting control rules.
UL
Underwriters Laboratories. Custom fixtures built with UL-listed parts avoid extra approval.
Daylighting
Using natural light to supplement or replace electric light.
Clerestory
A high band of windows between two roof levels.
Effective daylight depth
About 2.5 × h, where h is the height from the floor to the window head.
Light shelf
A horizontal shelf above eye level that bounces daylight onto the ceiling and deeper into the room while shading the lower glass.
Trap
A water-filled fitting that keeps sewer gas out.
Vent
A pipe open to the outside that releases gas and equalizes pressure so traps aren't siphoned dry.
Soil stack / waste stack
Vertical drains. A soil stack carries toilet (human) waste, and a waste stack carries only other waste.
Stack vent / vent stack
A stack vent is the part of a stack above the highest fixture. A vent stack is a separate vent pipe in multistory buildings.
Fixture branch
A pipe from a trap to a stack.
Cleanout
An access fitting for clearing a drain.

Main Points

Power & communications (p 16-10 to 16-11)

  • EMT is the most common conduit. The NEC sets conduit size by the number of wires, and a larger conduit needs a larger bend radius.
  • Telephone and data outlets go on the same plan as power. The designer locates them, and the engineer or contractor circuits them.
  • Communication wiring is low voltage, so it has fewer restrictions and may run without conduit.

Light sources — what to detail for (p 16-11 to 16-15)

  • Detailing considerations: size, weight, location, mounting method, and heat output.
  • Cove lights need continuous support. Recessed downlights need clearance above the ceiling.
  • Incandescent:
    • Pros: cheap, compact, easy to dim, frequent switching doesn't shorten life, warm color, easy to control with reflectors and lenses.
    • Cons: low efficacy, short life, high heat.
  • Halogen:
    • Whiter, more efficient light with steady color.
    • Runs hot and can shatter, so it must be enclosed by glass or a screen.
    • Available at 120 V or low voltage.
  • Low-voltage: small, with precise beams, good for lighting objects from far away. The transformer adds bulk and cost.
  • Fluorescent:
    • Efficient and linear.
    • Needs a ballast (leave room for it).
    • T8 and T5 replaced T12.
    • CFLs and GU24 lamps replace incandescent lamps.
  • Energy Star residential fixtures can't use the Edison screw base.
  • HID lamps light big spaces such as gyms, garages, and industrial areas. Metal halide is the best all-around HID, CMH gives the best color, and HPS is the most efficient but yellow.
  • Metal halide bulbs come clear (for optical control) or coated/diffuse (for better color).
  • LED: bright, long life, low energy, colors, and digital control, but higher cost. LED replacements exist for A, R, PAR, and MR lamps and for fluorescent tubes.
  • Neon and cold-cathode both need transformers and small mounting brackets.

Emergency lighting (p 16-12 to 16-13)

  • Governed by the IBC, NEC, Life Safety Code, and Canadian codes.
  • Required in:
    • Exit stairs and corridors.
    • Assembly spaces.
    • Educational and hazardous occupancies.
    • Large occupant loads.
  • Lighting and sign requirements:
    • At least 1 fc (10.8 lux) at the floor.
    • Exit signs at every exit door, with directional signs at turns.
    • Luminous egress path markings in some high-rises.
  • Remodels must reconnect or relocate emergency circuits and exit signs.

Lighting systems (p 16-15)

  • Recessed fixtures need plenum clearance. Troffers are usually fine, but large downlights may clash with ducts.
  • Choose low-clearance fixtures, and check both the drawings and the actual ceiling space.
  • Cove lighting:
    • Use fixtures made for coves.
    • Keep lamps out of sight lines from across the room.
    • Set lamps far enough from the surface to avoid hot spots.
    • Leave room for relamping.

Control devices (p 16-16 to 16-17)

  • Switching depends on:
    • The space's function.
    • How much individual control is needed.
    • Switch location (usually at the entry door).
    • Energy conservation.
    • The load per circuit.
  • Dimmed lights need their own circuit. Fluorescent dimming costs more.
  • Commercial voltages: incandescent runs on 120 V and fluorescent on 277 V, which is more efficient.
  • Dimmers alone don't save much energy, so automatic controls are required.
  • Daylight compensation needs the perimeter zone to get at least half its light from daylight for several hours a day. It can save up to 60%.
  • 90.1 automatic shutoff applies to buildings over 5000 ft², using one of:
    • Time-of-day controls (≤ 25,000 ft² and one floor per control).
    • Occupant sensors that turn lights off within 30 min.
    • A signal from another system.
  • Exceptions: 24-hour operations, patient care, and places where shutoff would endanger safety or security.
  • Per-space controls under 90.1:
    • Every enclosed space needs at least one control.
    • Classrooms, conference rooms, and break rooms need occupant sensors.
    • Each control covers at most 2500 ft² (spaces ≤ 10,000 ft²) or 10,000 ft² (larger spaces).
    • The override lasts up to 4 hr.
  • Separate controls are required for:
    • Display and accent lighting.
    • Hotel rooms (a master switch at the entry).
    • Task and under-cabinet lighting.
    • Nonvisual lighting (food warming).
    • Lighting for sale or demonstration.
  • Exterior lighting needs a photosensor or astronomical time switch.
  • Codes limit the watts per circuit, so large, bright spaces need several circuits.

Custom lighting & daylighting (p 16-18 to 16-19)

  • For custom fixtures, the designer sets the intent and a lighting designer details it. Use UL-listed parts, because fully custom fixtures need costly testing.
  • Lighting plus the cooling it requires uses 30–40% (up to 50%) of a commercial building's energy.
  • Daylighting saves energy and benefits occupants. Photocells cut perimeter lighting energy by 50–60%.
  • Designer strategies:
    • Open plans with window areas kept open.
    • Low partitions and interior glass.
    • Window coverings that let daylight in.
    • Reflective surfaces.
    • Electric lighting coordinated with daylight.
  • Variables:
    • Facade orientation.
    • Sky conditions (sun angle, clouds, time of day).
    • Glass area, head height, and transmittance.
    • Room and ground reflectance.
    • Obstructions such as overhangs and trees.
  • Problems:
    • Heat gain or loss.
    • Glare, which is worst on south sides.
    • Unbalanced light.
    • No energy saved unless the electric lights actually turn off.
  • Daylight drops off beyond 2.5h, so put enclosed rooms deeper in the floor plate.
  • Light shelves work best when they sit partly inside and partly outside the glass. The designer can add interior shelves in older buildings with tall windows.

Plumbing (p 16-20 to 16-21)

  • Plumbing has two parts: supply (pressurized, small pipes, easy to route) and drainage (gravity-driven, needs slope and vents, harder to route).
  • Traps are omitted only when a fixture has a built-in trap (toilets) or adjacent fixtures share one (a double sink).
  • Place fixtures as close as possible to existing stacks.
  • Drain slope is at least ¼ in/ft, or ⅛ in/ft for pipes over 3 in. The ceiling depth therefore limits how far a fixture can be from the stack.
  • Codes limit the trap-to-vent distance (IPC Table 16.2). Island sinks need special venting.
